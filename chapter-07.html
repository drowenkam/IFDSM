LEARNING OUTCOME §7.1

Age, weight, alcohol, drugs and sleep

Clinical key point

Weight gain or loss, ageing, alcohol use, sedatives and new illness can alter OSA severity after an initially successful OAT course. Reassess the whole patient, not just whether the device still fits.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 507 WORDS

Figure 7.1 — Factors modifying oral appliance therapy over time, tabulating each factor against its impact and the clinical considera‐ tion it generates: age (possible reduced adherence or fit issues in the elderly → assess retention, dentition and mucosal health), weight change (weight gain worsens OSA, weight loss improves fit and outcome → reassess appliance fit after weight change), alcohol use (increases airway collapse and reduces appliance efficacy → advise avoidance before bedtime), medication change (may alter muscle tone, sleep structure and appliance effect → monitor and adjust therapy as needed), and sleep hygiene (poor sleep reduces therapy benefit → encourage good routines and sufficient sleep).

Why it is examinable: this is the whole of 7.1 in one object, and its three-column structure — factor, impact, clinical action — is exactly the format to reproduce in a written answer.

The five modifiers in detail

ModifierEffect and management
AgeInfluences both adherence and effectiveness. Some studies show older adults have lower adher-ence, because changes in dentition and mucosal tissue make adaptation harder; others show no significant age-related difference. Overall, older adults can still benefit provided their oral health supports appliance retention — so the question to ask is not the patient's age but whether the dentition can still anchor the device
Weight changeWeight gain worsens OSA and may reduce appliance effectiveness by altering airway anatomy and appliance fit; weight loss improves outcomes. Significant fluctuation requires reassessment of both fit and efficacy — and is one of the specific triggers for repeat objective testing (Topic 6.3). Recall the quantitative relationship: 10% gain → ~32% AHI increase; 10% loss → ~26% AHI decrease
AlcoholConsumption before sleep exacerbates airway collapse and increases apnoea severity, undermin-ing the benefit of therapy. Even moderate intake may cause transient oxygen desaturation despite appliance use. Advise avoidance in the hours before bedtime
Medication changeDrugs affecting the central nervous system or muscle tone may alter airway patency or sleep archi-tecture, potentially reducing efficacy — sedatives, hypnotics, opioids, muscle relaxants. Emerging research explores combining pharmacotherapy with appliance therapy, but medication changes should be monitored closely for their effect on apnoea control. Ask about new medications at every review
Sleep hygiene and quantityGood hygiene and adequate duration support success by stabilising sleep architecture and reducing factors that worsen apnoea. Poor habits blunt the daytime alertness gains from therapy and potenti-ate apnoea severity, so consistent routines are recommended. Practically: a patient sleeping five hours will not feel the benefit of a well-titrated appliance, and may wrongly conclude that the appliance has failed

LEARNING OUTCOME §7.2

Longitudinal symptom documentation

Clinical key point

Record the original complaint, symptom scores when used, adherence, partner reports and any functional changes over time. Compare changes against the patient’s baseline rather than a generic “normal” pattern.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 303 WORDS

Documenting how the complaint evolves matters because it tracks treatment effectiveness, symptom progression and adherence simultaneously, and because it allows timely adjustment — reinforcing adherence and addressing emerging problems such as discomfort or incomplete symptom resolution. Two perspectives are needed.

AspectImportanceExamples of measures
Self-reported symptomsThe patient's perception of severity and of the effect of therapy — crucial insight into perceived health status and benefitEpworth Sleepiness Scale, sleep diaries, mobile applications
Sleep-observer reportsExternal confirmation and additional symptom in-sight — the bed partner or family member reports on snoring intensity, apnoeas and restless sleep, offering "objective-seeming" data that complement self-reportPartner observations, STOP-BANG items
Evolution trackingGuides treatment adjustment and encourages ad-herenceRegular follow-ups, app-based tracking, repeated administration of the same baseline questionnaires
Objective data integra-tionComplements subjective reporting to give a comprehensive assessmentAppliance compliance-monitor data, CPAP usage logs, polysomnography, oximetry

Monitoring changes in self-reported sleepiness correlates with long-term adherence and better outcomes, supporting continuous documentation. Typical yearly follow-up should include monitoring changes in sleep, blood pressure, weight loss or gain, behavioural risk factors, and re-evaluation of other metrics using previously employed question‐ naires.

HIGH-YIELD — W H Y THE B E D PA RTNERIS PA RTOF THE RECORD

The bed partner is often the only available reporter of the nocturnal phenotype, and — as Topic 6.6 notes — is frequently the person who perceives the benefit while the patient bears the side effects. Recording the partner's observations therefore serves three purposes: it detects recurrence of snoring or witnessed apnoeas before the patient notices; it documents benefit that the patient may under-report; and it identifies the moment when the perceived reason for treatment disappears, which is a strong predictor of discontinuation.

LEARNING OUTCOME §7.3

Treatment modification with progression

Clinical key point

Recurrent snoring or sleepiness, new cardiovascular morbidity, a large weight change or device failure should trigger dental and medical reassessment, often including repeat sleep testing after intervention.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 475 WORDS

Why modification is necessary

Progression of the disease. Sleep apnoea worsens over time, and the optimal advancement needed to keep the airway open may increase.

Long-term dental change. Prolonged appliance use produces dentoskeletal change — reduced overbite and overjet, altered incisor position. Critically, a forward shift of the lower teeth relative to the upper reduces the effective degree of mandibular advancement, putting the efficacy of treatment at risk. This is why appliances in long-term series had to be continuously advanced simply to compensate for occlusal drift.

Increasing side effects. A greater degree of protrusion often increases the incidence of jaw stiffness, tooth discomfort and dry mouth, requiring careful adjustment to balance effectiveness against comfort.

How the appliance is modified

Titration. Adjust the advancement in small increments, repeating until the most effective level is found that still manages symptoms while minimising side effects.

Regular follow-up. Check-ups with both dentist and sleep specialist to monitor the progress of the apnoea and the effects of the appliance on the jaw and teeth.

Appliance re-adjustment. Based on reassessment, adjust the protrusion level; with significant progression this may mean a larger advancement or a switch to a different appliance type.

Monitoring for side effects. Assess short- and long-term side effects at each follow-up and modify settings to relieve discomfort.

Key considerations: the optimal mandibular position is unique to each patient and must be determined by careful repeated titration; construction, fitting and adjustment should be performed by a dentist with expertise in appliance therapy; and the process is always a balance between therapeutic effect and side effects.

Modification of other therapies for progressive SDB

The same principle — a dynamic, personalised, ongoing plan — applies across modalities, because SDB worsens with age and weight gain and can be exacerbated by other medical conditions.

StrategyDetail
Adjusting PAP therapyPressure settings on CPAP or BPAP need regular fine-tuning to match the current degree of disease. Home and in-laboratory polysomnography diagnose SDB, determine severity and evaluate treatment effectiveness over time
Lifestyle and behavioural modificationWeight management, which significantly reduces severity by decreasing upper airway col-lapsibility; sleep position training to avoid the supine posture; and avoidance of alcohol, opioids and other medications that worsen SDB
Managing concomitant conditionsOptimising treatment of coexisting heart failure, asthma or chronic lung disease; treating nasal obstruction — nasal valve collapse, allergic rhinitis — with nasal steroids or surgery to improve airflow
Novel and advanced ther-apiesHypoglossal nerve stimulation as a salvage option for PAP-intolerant patients; maxillary or palatal expansion to increase airway volume and reduce collapsibility; myofunctional therapy to improve upper airway muscle strength and endurance
Recognising progressionAn integrated approach is required, often combining multiple therapies. Note also that some processes are reversible: treatment of SDB can reduce inflammatory oedema and increase pharyngeal size, improving the condition's trajectory

LEARNING OUTCOME §7.4

Annual device and clinical review

Clinical key point

Periodic appointments should review tooth and periodontal health, occlusion, TMJ, appliance retention, adjustment capacity, fractures, hygiene, adherence and the need for replacement. Follow-up intervals depend on risk and local standards.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 1,510 WORDS

K E Y TA K E AWAY

Ensuring the long-term success of an oral appliance hinges on a thorough yearly evaluation of its condition and fit, combined with clear reinforcement of proper cleaning, handling and storage routines.

1. Appliance condition assessment

Surface integrity— check for cracks, crazing or rough spots that could irritate the oral tissues.

Material wear— note discolouration, thinning or deformation of the acrylic or thermoplastic.

Hardware functionality— where metal components are present (clasps, hinges, screws), ensure they are free of corrosion and move smoothly without binding.

Also assess for bacterial and fungal growth, and replace according to the patient's needs.

Reiterate to the patient:"It's important that your appliance remains intact. Even small cracks can harbour bacteria and compromise effectiveness."

2. Fit and stability evaluation

Seating accuracy— confirm the appliance fully engages the intended undercuts, or rests on the occlusal surfaces, without rocking.

Retention— gently tug to verify the device resists displacement during normal oral function.

Occlusal contacts— ensure the appliance does not interfere with the bite or cause premature contacts.

Verify appliance efficacy and occlusal stability, and that previously resolved symptoms such as snoring and daytime sleepiness remain resolved.

Communicate clearly:"A stable fit keeps you comfortable and ensures the appliance does its job. If you ever feel looseness, schedule an interim visit — don't wait until next year."

3. Reinforcing daily care

Cleaning: rinse immediately after removal to wash away saliva and debris; brush gently with a soft toothbrush and a non-abrasive, fragrance-free cleanser, avoiding toothpaste; disinfect weekly in a denture-safe soak per manufacturer instructions.

Handling: always remove over a soft surface or a basin filled with water to prevent accidental drop damage; avoid hot water and direct sunlight, which warp the appliance.

Storage: store dry in the ventilated case when not in use — never wrapped in tissue or loose in a pocket; keep away from pets, children and heat sources.

Emphasise:"Consistent daily care helps prevent odour, staining and bacterial growth. A few minutes each day will save you discomfort and costly repairs later."

4. Scheduling and next steps

Confirm the patient understands all aspects of appliance care and fit; offer to demonstrate in-office cleaning techniques if needed.

Assess adherence and comfort, and screen for TMJ-related, intraoral tissue-related and occlusal side effects, damage to teeth or restorations, and appliance problems. Where side effects are noted, document their presence, management and manner of resolution, and communicate relevant subjective and objective findings to the medical provider and the general dentist.

Recommend the patient contact the clinic immediately if discomfort, looseness or damage occurs.

Schedule the next annual review, reminding the patient that an earlier check is available on request.

Notify the medical provider if the patient terminates therapy.

Appliance replacement

Patients needing a replacement appliance may need a comprehensive dental sleep medicine evaluation before fabrication of a new device, and this is required if the patient was treated previously by another dentist. Communicate with the medical provider early to determine what circumstances would trigger a referral before replacement; the provider should be notified of the request, given the opportunity to reassess, and allowed to decide whether efficacy of the new appliance needs verification. If the patient was previously treated effectively, the new appliance can be set to the same protrusion setting — though it may still require calibration to restore the therapeutic position.

SELF-TEST — TOPIC 7

1. A patient stable on appliance therapy for four years returns with recurrent snoring and daytime sleepiness. Work through your assessment.

I would consider four categories. Disease progression— OSA worsens with age and weight gain, so the previously therapeut‐ ic advancement may now be insufficient; check weight change, blood pressure and new comorbidities. Occlusal drift— a for‐ ward shift of the lower teeth relative to the upper reduces the effective advancement, so the appliance may be at the same setting but delivering less protrusion; compare current overjet and overbite against the baseline records and study casts. Appli‐ ance failure— cracks, crazing, material fatigue, worn or corroded hardware, or loss of retention. Behavioural and pharma‐ cological modifiers— new alcohol intake, new sedating medication, reduced sleep duration or deteriorated sleep hygiene, and objective adherence data. I would then re-titrate as indicated, arrange objective reassessment, and refer back to the sleep physician for verification, remembering that recurrence of symptoms is a specific indication for repeat objective testing.

2. Why must the appliance be advanced over time even when the disease has not progressed?

Because prolonged appliance use produces progressive occlusal change: the lower teeth move anteriorly and the upper teeth distally, so at a fixed appliance setting the effective mandibular advancement steadily reduces. In long-term series appliances had to be continuously advanced simply to compensate for the shift in dental occlusion. This is a genuinely uncomfortable feature of the therapy — the side effect erodes the treatment effect, and compensating for it requires more protrusion, which in turn accelerates the side effect. It is a strong argument for minimising titration to the least effective advancement, for a morning occlusal guide, and for careful annual comparison against baseline records.

3. What do you assess at the annual review?

Four domains. Appliance condition: surface integrity (cracks, crazing, rough spots), material wear (discolouration, thinning, deformation), hardware function (corrosion, smooth movement), and bacterial or fungal growth. Fit and stability: seating ac‐ curacy without rocking, retention on gentle traction, occlusal contacts and absence of premature contact, plus verification of continuing efficacy and occlusal stability. Care practices: reinforce cleaning, handling and storage. Clinical status: adherence and comfort; TMJ, soft tissue and occlusal side effects; damage to teeth or restorations; changes in sleep, blood pressure, weight and behavioural risk factors; and re-administration of the baseline questionnaires. Document any side effects and their management, communicate findings to the medical provider and general dentist, and schedule the next review while making clear that earlier review is available on request.

4. How do alcohol and new medications affect appliance therapy, and what do you advise?

Alcohol before sleep reduces pharyngeal dilator muscle tone and blunts arousal responses, exacerbating airway collapse and increasing apnoea severity; even moderate intake can cause transient oxygen desaturation despite appliance use. Advise avoidance in the hours before bed. New medications acting on the central nervous system or muscle tone — sedatives, hypnotics, opioids, muscle relaxants — may reduce airway patency or alter sleep architecture and so reduce appliance efficacy; some antidepressants additionally induce bruxism or restless legs. I therefore take a medication history at every review, and where a change coincides with symptom recurrence I consider it as a cause before advancing the appliance further.
