LEARNING OUTCOME §3.1

History of OAT

Clinical key point

Oral appliance therapy (OAT) has evolved from simple protrusive devices to custom, titratable appliances with documented calibration. Historical designs matter mainly when explaining why device fit and adjustability affect outcomes.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 384 WORDS

Figure / era Date Contribution

George Catlin19th c.The first to suggest that breathing affects sleep quality — that breathing through the nose rather than the mouth produces more restful, better quality sleep, translating into better daytime function and better general health
Pierre Robin1903Described the monobloc for the treatment of glossoptosis (a posteriorly displaced tongue obstructing the airway). Some 30 years later the appliance was used to reposition the mandible
The quiet period1903– 1950sApproximately 50 years of very little development in oral appliances for SDB
Cartwright & Samelson1982Described the tongue retaining device, using suction to hold the tongue forward — historically the second most common appliance type
Meier-Ewert et al.1985Described an intraoral protraction device for sleep apnoea — a key step towards using mandibular advancement to treat OSA. The work of the 1980s was the catalyst for the modern field
Modern era1990s–Focus consolidated on mandibular advancement appliances. Early case reports gave way to randomised controlled trials confirming efficacy for mild-to-moderate OSA, and more recently for severe OSA. Soft palate lifters are no longer used; tongue retaining devices are used only rarely
DEFINITIONS — THE AADSMTERMINOLOGYSET

Oral appliance (OA)— a device intended to protrude and stabilise the mandible in order to maintain a patent airway during sleep.

Custom OA— fabricated using digital or physical impressions or models of the patient's oral structures; not prefabricated; made of biocompatible material; engaging both the upper and lower arches.

Non-custom OA ("boil-and-bite")— prefabricated and only partially modified to the patient's oral structures.

Titratable OA— allows a varying amount of mandibular protrusion (the analogue of CPAP titration).

Non-titratable OA— holds the mandible in a single protrusive position with no change possible.

Definition of an effective oral appliance (AADSM 2019). The appliance has a mechanism allowing the mandible to be advanced in increments of 1 mm or less, with a protrusive adjustment range of at least 5 mm; reversal of the advancement must be possible; the protrusive setting must be verifiable; the appliance is suitable for placement and removal by the patient or caregiver; and it maintains a stable retentive relationship to the teeth, implants or edentulous ridge, retaining the prescribed setting during use. It should also be FDA-cleared (or equivalent).

Mechanism of OAT

Clinical key point

Mandibular advancement generally enlarges or stabilises the upper airway by advancing the jaw and associated soft tissues; the anatomical response is variable and cannot be inferred from a single cephalometric measurement.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 376 WORDS

Tongue retaining device (TRD)

A pliable silicone bulb fitted over a protruded tongue and held by suction, supported additionally by the teeth and soft tissues. Holding the tongue in a forward posture prevents it collapsing back into the oropharyngeal airway — the commonest site of collapse. Indicated where dental factors preclude a mandibular advancement appliance; evidence for efficacy is insufficient, and it is used very seldom today.

Mandibular advancement device (MAD)

The most commonly used and best researched device. Dentally retained, either one-piece or a dual-component design with upper and lower retainers connected by a coupling mechanism, designed to reposition the mandible forward and support that protrusive posture through the night.

Mode of action: an increase in the patency of the upper airway during sleep; provision of a stable and consistent anterior position of the mandible; advancement of the tongue; and possibly an increase in upper airway volume and/or a change in airway shape, with altered genioglossus muscle activity.

HIGH-YIELD — THE MECHANISM , INSEQUENCE

Mandibular protrusion advances the jaw and attached musculature, enlarging the airway.

Velopharyngeal advancement: increased tension in the palatoglossal and palatopharyngeal muscles draws the soft palate anteriorly and inferiorly, displacing the velopharynx forward. This is why an appliance that acts on the mandible improves a palatal-level obstruction.

Tongue base advancement: increased tension in and stabilisation of the suprahyoid and genioglossus muscles, preventing the tongue falling back; the hyoid is stabilised by forward mandibular movement.

Structural change to the pharynx: the retroglossal airway enlarges, and — the key point — the airway enlarges prin‐ cipally in its lateral dimension at the velopharyngeal level, through traction on the soft tissue connections between the pharynx and the mandibular ramus.

Net physiological effect: improved passive pharyngeal anatomy and reduced pharyngeal collapsibility, in a dose-de‐ pendent manner, without systematic change to genioglossus muscle function, loop gain or arousal threshold.

Figure 3.1 — The two appliance classes: mandibular advancement devices (left), which reposition the mandible forward, and the tongue retaining device (right), which holds the tongue anteriorly by suction.

Why it is examinable: the TRD survives in examinations chiefly as the answer to "what would you offer a patient with insufficient dentition to retain a MAD?" — while noting that evidence for its efficacy is insufficient.

Indications and contraindications

Clinical key point

Custom, titratable OAT is a supported option for patients who prefer it or cannot tolerate PAP, following medical diagnosis. Assess retention, periodontal stability, protrusive range, TMD symptoms and expectations before choosing a device.

PAP therapies

Clinical key point

PAP usually reduces respiratory-event frequency more than OAT when worn. Mask fit, pressure intolerance and adherence can alter real-world benefit; the medical team should troubleshoot PAP rather than dismiss it after one difficult night.

A CPAP machine has three parts: a mask or other interface fitting over the nose, or nose and mouth, with straps; a tube connecting the mask to the motor; and a motor blowing air into the tube.

Side effects. Mostly benign and mild: nasal congestion, dryness of the oral and nasal cavity, dry mouth, sore throat, aerophagia (excessive swallowing of air), epistaxis, sneezing, sinusitis. Rare but serious adverse effects have been reported: pneumocephalus, pulmonary barotrauma, raised intraocular pressure and subcutaneous emphysema. Increased middle ear pressure may cause otic barotrauma — a pressure differential between the middle ear and the environment causing pain, tinnitus and vertigo, with middle ear inflammation and conductive hearing loss; pressure transmitted to the inner ear can cause sensorineural loss, since a sudden middle ear pressure rise transmitted via the Eustachian tube may disrupt the oval or round window, damaging cochlear membranes or producing a perilymph fistula.

Titration and mask selection

Titration establishes the minimum pressure at which snoring ceases and apnoeic events are absent. Start at 4 cmH 2 O in an adult and titrate to the lowest effective pressure, to a maximum of about 20 cmH 2 O.

Nasal masks are effective in mild, moderate and severe apnoea. Oronasal (full face) masks require significantly higher pressures in moderate-to-severe disease: at higher pressures the mouth is forced open by the jaw being pushed down, compromising efficacy; leakage is greater than with nasal pillows; and simultaneous nasal and oral pressure application prevents the necessary increase in transmural pressure needed to open the airway. Therefore, when changing from a nasal to an oronasal mask, repeat titration is required, especially in moderate-to-severe disease. Nasal or intranasal interfaces are suggested over oronasal or oral interfaces for routine initiation.

Heated humidification is suggested to reduce side effects — dry mouth and throat, nasal discharge and congestion, dry or bleeding nose, sinus pain, headache, sore throat, hoarse voice and reduced smell. The trade-offs are "rain- out" (condensation in the circuit and on the face), increased ongoing costs and maintenance.

Modified pressure profile PAP(lower pressure during expiration) may increase comfort in poorly adherent patients or those struggling during in-laboratory titration, but no clinically significant difference has been demonstrated to support its routine use over standard PAP.

What to do when CPAP fails — a structured answer

This is a classic viva. Work through it in four stages rather than jumping to alternatives.

Was treatment of the OSA necessary in the first place? Check the study: is the OSA severe enough to mandate therapy? What level of study was it — level 1 or 2? Were the events apnoeas or hypopnoeas? Were there desaturations? Is there significant fatigue or daytime sleepiness? Are there compelling comorbidities?

Have the merits of treatment been explained? Why treat, and why is CPAP the usual first choice — it is the gold standard and almost universally successful if tolerated, though not cheap. Explain the pathophysiology and the organ systems involved. Set the expectation that the patient may feel substantially better. Ensure the patient appreciates that alternatives are neither free of complications nor guaranteed to succeed.

Was the CPAP trial properly conducted, to establish true intolerability? Start slowly and build up. Daily ther‐ apy review through remote monitoring platforms. Were there regular mask changes and gradual pressure increases? Was the patient treated at a tolerable pressure before aspiring to therapeutic targets?

Only then move to alternatives, multidisciplinary. Weight loss; nasal EPAP valves (Provent ~8 cmH 2 O,

Theravent 2–6 cmH 2 O — expensive and not very comfortable); mandibular advancement splint; ENT assessment

and surgery; positional therapy; combination therapy; and referral to a high-powered specialist unit for trial therapies such as mandibular advancement surgery, hypoglossal nerve stimulation or tracheostomy. Some patients are genuine treatment failures in whom nothing works well.

Surgery

Clinical key point

Surgical options depend on anatomy, patient goals and objective disease characteristics. No operation guarantees cure; residual OSA and objective reassessment remain relevant.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 818 WORDS

Surgery is comparatively uncommon in the OSA pathway. Its role is either adjunctive(alongside CPAP or OAT — for example nasal surgery to enable CPAP tolerance) or salvage(replacing CPAP or OAT). Non-invasive options should be offered first. The organising triad is expand, advance, stiffen.

Surgical decision-making depends on apnoea severity, anatomy and level of obstruction, CPAP compliance, patient expectation and comorbidity. Drug-induced sleep endoscopy (DISE) is the principal investigation for surgical

candidates, identifying the site (the VOTE classification: V elum, O ropharynx, T ongue base, E piglottis) and the pattern and degree of collapse. Anteroposterior collapse is the easiest to treat; lateral or circumferential collapse generally requires MMA. DISE can also be used to observe directly whether mandibular advancement opens the airway. Fitness for surgery matters — a roughly 4% adverse event rate (cardiovascular events, postoperative bleeding) — and patients must be reminded that surgery may not completely cure the problem.

Figure 3.3 — Sequence of treatment for obstructive sleep apnoea, with the shaded boxes marking the dentist's role. The pathway runs from presentation with snoring (with or without witnessed apnoeas or daytime sleepiness) → referral to physician → physician request and interpretation of overnight polysomnography or sleep testing → severity stratification (snore/no OSA · mild-to-moderate · severe) → behavioural treatment with weight loss, side sleep position and avoidance of sedatives, either alone or concurrent with CPAP → choice of oral appliance or CPAP → clinical assessment, insertion and titration of the appliance with behavioural treatment → subjective or objective reassessment at maximal comfortable mandibular advancement, with the option of a portable monitor for titration purposes → referral back to the physician for a sleep specialist or family physician review, with overnight polysomnography or home sleep testing → annual follow-up by the dentist → physician follow-up approximately every five years, with reassessment if the appliance is in‐ adequate.

LEARNING OUTCOME §3.6

Positional and behavioural therapies

Clinical key point

Weight management, positional therapy, alcohol reduction and sleep-hygiene measures may support a treatment plan, but behavioural advice alone may be insufficient for clinically significant OSA.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 490 WORDS

Weight reduction

AspectDetail
Rationale10% weight gain predicts a ~32% AHI increase; 10% weight loss predicts a ~26% AHI decrease. Mechanisms: reduced nasopharyngeal collapsibility; reduced mechanical loading, particularly from parapharyngeal fat pads; reduced central adiposity, lowering adipokines acting on the CNS and so enhancing neuromuscular control of pharyngeal calibre; and increased lung volume, increasing caudal tracheal traction on the pharynx
TargetBMI <30, though the effect is greatest in patients with a high starting BMI (35–45)
MethodLifestyle and behavioural treatment with dietitian, exercise physiologist and nutritionist. A very low calorie diet plus exercise plus supervised lifestyle counselling produces weight loss, reduced waist circumference and AHI reduction. Arguably first-line for mild OSA in adults
PharmacologicalGLP-1 receptor agonists (liraglutide/Saxenda; tirzepatide) and phentermine. Sibutramine is no longer used because of myocardial infarction and stroke risk and inferior effect compared with CPAP; orlistat (inhibiting gastrointestinal lipase to reduce fat absorption) is an alternative
Advantages / disadvant-agesFeasible, low cost, potentially curative, and improves other obesity-related cardiovascular risk factors — but difficult to achieve and, more importantly, to sustain

Positional therapy

Principle: promotes side sleeping instead of supine sleeping. Snoring severity and AHI are greater supine; in children the lowest AHI is often prone.

Indication: positional data from the PSG establish the indication. Strictly defined, positional OSA means a non- supine AHI <5; the therapeutic goal is to reduce total sleep time spent supine to zero. Also requires a motivated patient and financial resources.

Methods: older techniques — tennis or golf ball in a sock or a bra worn backwards, body pillows, special shirts and vests — with poor long-term adherence due to discomfort and ineffectiveness. Newer smart devices (e.g. NightShift) detect body position and deliver a vibratory stimulus; note that these generally provide no objective compliance data.

Efficacy: reduces snoring frequency and rate in most non-apnoeic snorers (AHI <5), but not in those with OSA. A reasonable alternative in mild-to-moderate OSA and where CPAP adherence is poor. Compared with OAT, positional therapy achieves similar AHI reduction in positional OSA, but OAT gives a greater improvement in sleepiness and in non-supine AHI. Less sustainable long term; requires follow-up.

Sleep hygiene and behavioural therapy

Sleep behavioural therapy encompasses sleep hygiene, sleeping position, weight control and avoidance of sedatives including alcohol. Provide patients with explanations and written information on all of these, alongside the device being considered and all alternatives. Lifestyle modification is recommended, though evidence specifically quantifying the effect of sleep hygiene on OSA remains limited.

Cognitive behavioural therapy

CBT changes unhelpful thought patterns and behaviours, developing problem-solving skills and promoting relaxation and stress management. It is the recommended treatment for insomnia and is particularly valuable in comorbid insom‐ nia and sleep apnoea (COMISA), where both conditions must be treated — reducing the AHI alone will not resolve the patient's complaint. CBT and motivational enhancement also improve PAP adherence.

Clinical key point

Newer pharmacologic or stimulation treatments have selected indications, constraints and changing evidence. Avoid presenting endotype-targeted investigational approaches as routine dental prescribing.

Mean disease alleviation

Clinical key point

Mean disease alleviation is a conceptual function of efficacy and adherence. It explains why a less potent but more consistently used treatment can offer meaningful benefit; it is not a universal fixed percentage for every patient.

Figure 3.4 — Treatment effectiveness profile of CPAP versus an oral appliance. Efficacy (y axis) reflects the ability of the treatment to prevent obstructive breathing events while it is physically applied. Compliance (x axis) reflects the hours the treatment is applied, over the total sleep time when obstructive events can occur. "Effectiveness" requires both, and the shaded area — the product of the two — reflects likely health outcomes. The schematic illustrates an oral appliance that is only half as efficacious as CPAP but has two-fold greater compliance, resulting in equivalent effectiveness.

Why it is examinable: this figure is the answer to the apparent paradox that runs through the whole examination — how a less efficacious treatment can produce equal health outcomes. Reported MDA is approximately 51% for oral appliances versus 50% for CPAP. Draw this diagram in a viva and you will be understood immediately.

Practical implications. MDA allows direct comparison of therapies by accounting for both biological effect and patient behaviour. High efficacy does not guarantee high effectiveness if compliance is poor; a treatment with moderate efficacy but high compliance may yield better real-world outcomes. The definition and measurement of compliance are critical, because inconsistent definitions limit comparability of MDA across studies — a legitimate criticism to raise if asked to critique the concept. Note also the emerging refinement of effective AHI, which accounts not only for the AHI on treatment but for the AHI during the untreated hours.

LEARNING OUTCOME §3.11

Guidelines and evidence-based care

Clinical key point

The AASM/AADSM guideline favors a custom, titratable appliance over non-custom devices when OAT is prescribed for OSA and recommends follow-up testing and periodic reviews with both dentist and sleep provider.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 2,197 WORDS

Why evidence-based dentistry

To provide appropriate care, reduce inappropriate care and increase the effectiveness of care, by promoting the use of the best available evidence to inform clinical decision-making.

DEFINITIONS

Dentistry— the evaluation, diagnosis, prevention and/or treatment (non-surgical, surgical, or reversible and irreversible procedures) of diseases, disorders, irregularities or conditions of the oral cavity, maxillofacial area and/or adjacent and associated structures, and their impact on the human body.

Evidence-based dentistry (EBD)— an approach to oral health care that requires the judicious integration of systematic assessments of clinically relevant scientific evidence, relating to the patient's oral and medical condition and history, with the dentist's clinical expertise and the patient's treatment needs, values and circumstances.

The five steps of EBD

Question. Define a clinically relevant and focused question where evidence may promote the patient's oral health.

Search for information. Systematically search for the best evidence across all studies, grade the strength of evidence using predefined criteria, and identify gaps.

Appraisal. Critically appraise the evidence for validity, using critical evaluation tools and an understanding of research methods.

Application. Apply the results to patient care, considering clinical skills and the patient's preferences, values and conditions.

Evaluation. Assess health care outcomes and patient-reported outcomes to inform future patient care.

The parallel medical formulation is the six-A cycle: ask, acquire, appraise, aggregate, apply, assess. Either is accept‐ able; the three core components of evidence-based practice are best available evidence, clinical expertise and patient values.

Best practice in dental sleep medicine

Obtain written informed consent before treatment

Keep records: study models, radiographs, photographs

Satisfy the relevant regulator and professional association policies and codes of conduct (e.g. Dental Board, ADA, NZDA)

Maintain ongoing education

Avoid conflicts of interest — appliance selection should never be determined solely by insurer requirements or commercial relationships

Hold appropriate and recognised training and experience

Be aware of driving guidelines and fitness-to-drive obligations

Guidelines exist to standardise care, reduce unwarranted variation and ensure that the most effective and safe interventions are used. They are developed by aggregating and critically evaluating the best available evidence — empirical evidence from randomised controlled trials, cohort and case-control studies, together with non-research evidence from case reports, established scientific principles and expert opinion. Best practice is the highest achievable standard for a procedure, technique or intervention, informed by scientific evidence and leading to optimal outcomes; evidence-based practice is the mechanism by which best practice is identified and maintained, and its benefits are improved outcomes, enhanced quality, greater efficiency, reduced cost and minimised error and risk.

SELF-TEST — TOPIC 3

1. Give the AADSM definition of an effective oral appliance.

A mechanism allowing the mandible to be advanced in increments of 1 mm or less, with a protrusive adjustment range of at least 5 mm; reversal of advancement must be possible; the protrusive setting must be verifiable; the appliance must be suitable for placement and removal by the patient or caregiver; and it must maintain a stable retentive relationship to the teeth, implants or edentulous ridge and retain the prescribed setting during use. It should be FDA-cleared or the local equivalent. Note that a boil-and-bite device fails several of these criteria — it cannot be reliably titrated, its setting is not verifiable, and its retention is poor.

2. Explain how a mandibular appliance improves an obstruction at the level of the soft palate.

Mandibular protrusion increases tension in the palatoglossus and palatopharyngeus, which draws the soft palate anteriorly and inferiorly, displacing the velopharynx forward. In addition, traction on the soft tissue connections between the pharynx and the mandibular ramus widens the airway laterally at the velopharyngeal level — this lateral dimensional change is the predominant mechanism. So the appliance is not simply pulling the tongue forward; it remodels the whole velo-oropharyngeal segment.

3. Define efficacy, compliance and mean disease alleviation, and use them to explain why CPAP and oral appliances produce similar health outcomes.

Efficacy is the maximum potential benefit under ideal conditions — the AHI reduction achieved while the treatment is physically applied. Compliance is the proportion of prescribed therapy actually used. Mean disease alleviation is their product, and represents true real-world effectiveness. CPAP has roughly twice the efficacy of an oral appliance but substantially lower adherence; the appliance's greater nightly use (~6.5 versus ~5.2 hours) offsets its lower efficacy, giving similar MDA figures of about 51% and 50% respectively. High efficacy therefore does not guarantee high effectiveness. A fair criticism of MDA is that inconsistent definitions of compliance limit comparability across studies.

4. Your patient has failed CPAP. Structure your approach.

Four stages. First, question whether treatment was necessary: review the study level, event type, desaturation, severity, symptoms and comorbidities. Second, confirm the merits of treatment were explained — why treat, why CPAP is usually first, the pathophysiology, the likely symptomatic benefit, and that alternatives are neither complication-free nor guaranteed. Third, verify the CPAP trial was properly conducted before accepting true intolerance: slow build-up, remote therapy review, mask changes, gradual pressure increases, and treating at a tolerable rather than immediately therapeutic pressure. Only fourth, move to alternatives in a multidisciplinary setting: weight loss, EPAP valves, mandibular advancement splint, ENT assessment and surgery, positional therapy, combination therapy, and specialist-unit trial therapies including mandibular advancement surgery, hypoglossal nerve stimulation and tracheostomy. Acknowledge that some patients are genuine treatment failures.

5. What is the role of surgery, and what investigation guides it?

Surgery is either adjunctive — alongside CPAP or OAT, for example nasal surgery to enable CPAP tolerance — or salvage, replacing them. Non-invasive options are offered first. The organising principle is expand, advance, stiffen: nasal surgery, soft tissue procedures (UPPP and modifications, tongue base reduction), skeletal surgery (maxillomandibular advancement, distraction, SARPE/DOME, genioplasty — generally better outcomes than soft tissue surgery), neurostimulation, and bariatric surgery, with tracheostomy as a last resort. Drug-induced sleep endoscopy guides selection by identifying the site (VOTE: velum, oropharynx, tongue base, epiglottis) and the pattern and degree of collapse: anteroposterior collapse is easiest to treat, while lateral or circumferential collapse generally requires MMA. DISE can also confirm directly whether mandibular advancement opens the airway.

6. Which patients are suitable for hypoglossal nerve stimulation, and which are excluded?

Suitable: BMI <35, AHI 20–50, and non-concentric collapse of the retropalatal airway, typically in a patient refractory to or intolerant of CPAP. Excluded: complete concentric collapse on drug-induced sleep endoscopy, and grade 3–4 tonsils. The device comprises a stimulation lead to cranial nerve XII and a sensing lead in the fourth intercostal space to detect inspiration. Outcomes include reduced AHI, ODI and ESS, improved SAQLI and FOSQ, and adherence of around 86% — better than CPAP. Availability is jurisdiction-dependent.

7. A patient with primary snoring and no daytime symptoms asks you to make an appliance. What must happen first?

A sleep physician must exclude OSA. Snoring cannot be distinguished from OSA clinically, one third of people with OSA do not snore and one third of snorers have no OSA, and patients who snore must not be treated in the absence of a diagnosis. Once the absence of OSA is confirmed, an oral appliance is appropriate first-line therapy for snoring in a dentate patient, and no further referral is needed for the appliance itself. Conservative measures should also be addressed — weight loss, reduced alcohol, positional therapy where snoring is supine-dominant. Follow-up sleep testing is not indicated for primary snoring unless symptoms worsen or fail to resolve.

8. Name the five steps of evidence-based dentistry.

Question — define a focused, clinically relevant question. Search — systematically identify the best evidence and grade its strength. Appraisal — critically appraise validity using critical evaluation tools. Application — apply results considering clinical skills and patient preferences, values and conditions. Evaluation — assess health and patient-reported outcomes to inform future care. The three core components integrated throughout are best available evidence, clinical expertise and patient values.
