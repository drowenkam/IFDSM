LEARNING OUTCOME §6.1

Chief complaint and adjustment

Clinical key point

Return to the original complaint and the baseline sleep study. Ask whether the patient’s snoring, sleepiness, awakenings and functional goals improved, and whether adherence supports a fair treatment trial.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 713 WORDS

Subjective measures

The dentist should monitor and collect information on the resolution of the patient's sleep disorder symptoms in order to assess efficacy and determine the optimal titration of the appliance: reduced snoring, improved daytime symptoms, and improvement in the specific complaint the patient originally brought. Within 30 days after insertion, and at regular intervals thereafter, the patient should be evaluated and reassessed for signs and symptoms using the same questionnaires and tools used at the initial assessment— otherwise change cannot be quantified.

Objective measures

HIGH-YIELD — THE SCOPE - O F - PRACTICERULEFOROBJECTIVED ATA

If the dentist can obtain objective data, for example with a portable monitor or pulse oximeter, this information should be used for titration purposes only— not for follow-up assessment and not for diagnosis. The dentist working with portable monitors for titration should be able to interpret the results accurately, which means monitoring mainly the changes in res‐ piratory sleep parameters with treatment. The dentist should not use this equipment for diagnosis or as a formal assessment; it is the role of the physician to use portable monitors for check-ups or reassessments. Any decisions about the use of home sleep apnoea tests or pulse oximetry, and the resulting objective data, should be made in concert with the patient, the treating medical provider and the qualified dentist.

How to advance — and when to revert

Improvements in AHI are dose-dependent on the amount of mandibular advancement, so advancement is the primary lever. A representative published protocol:

At initiation, the mandible is set at 50% of the patient's maximum protrusion.

The patient accustoms to this protrusive position over a 2-week period.

Patients are then allowed to adjust the appliance over a 6-week period.

Where symptoms persist — snoring, excessive daytime sleepiness, apnoeas or hypopnoeas — patients are instructed to advance the mandible each night by one to two increments (0.2–0.4 mm).

Adjustment continues until symptoms are adequately improved (no signs of apnoea, hypopnoea or snoring) or until further protrusion causes discomfort.

The vertical dimension is kept constant throughout the follow-up period.

Practical points from clinical practice: the titration period for an appliance is around 5 months, longer than for CPAP, but once tolerated compliance is better; sufficient time is needed for the TMJ to accustom to a more advanced position. Symptoms typically improve before the AHI does. Always leave some leeway rather than going straight to 75%. Nylon appliances are easier to titrate because the scale is visible on the device, whereas acrylic requires turning the screw incrementally. Titration may be performed by the patient or the dentist. Completion of titration usually takes 2–3 months. Home oximetry and other objective measures can be used to support titration, and a wearable home test can be arranged once you believe the titration is adequate. Sequential polysomnographic studies facilitate titration. Note that in reported long-term series, all appliances had been continuously adjusted with more advancement in order to compensate for the shift in dental occlusion— a sobering observation to which Topic 10 returns.

Reverting during titration

StepAction
AssessAt each follow-up, ask about the chief complaint (excessive daytime sleepiness, recurrent snoring) and assess for side effects — tooth pain, jaw stiffness, TMJ discomfort

Why revert? To mitigate or resolve unacceptable side effects such as persistent tooth pain, jaw discomfort or TMJ symptoms; to balance efficacy against tolerance in finding the optimal advancement; and to avoid prematurely abandoning titration because of minor side effects, which would lead to a suboptimal outcome. The patient's subjective feedback and history of discomfort guide the process, gradual increments balance efficacy against tolerance, and objective data from sleep studies or home monitoring inform decisions about returning to previous settings.

LEARNING OUTCOME §6.2

Symptoms and side-effects monitoring

Clinical key point

At every adjustment, review oral discomfort, jaw pain, occlusal change, retention and device function. A patient may be improving symptomatically while side effects are eroding their ability to use the appliance.

Clinical approach: record and discuss symptoms at each visit; reassure about common transient side effects and educate on managing them; adjust the appliance or refer for specialised care as needed; monitor dental and skeletal change with periodic assessment, especially in long-term users, to prevent irreversible problems; and coordinate care with sleep physicians for optimal management and for discussion of alternative therapy if the appliance is intolerable or ineffective.

LEARNING OUTCOME §6.3

Referral for objective assessment

Clinical key point

The treating medical provider should verify efficacy objectively after the therapeutic position is established. Additional medical testing is warranted when symptoms recur, disease risk changes or residual disease is suspected.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 340 WORDS
HIGH-YIELD — WHYSUBJECTIVEFEEDBACKALONEISINSUFFICIENT

Two facts make this non-negotiable. First, oral appliances may worsen OSA in some patients. Second, there are known placebo effects with this type of therapy — a patient may feel better while the AHI is unchanged or worse. Referral of the OSA patient for follow-up evaluation by the referring physician, with polysomnography or sleep studies, is therefore re‐ quired to verify treatment efficacy and benefit.

Reason to refer for objective testingDetail
Confirming efficacySubjective feedback alone is insufficient to determine whether the appliance is working optimally; objective testing confirms the device achieves the desired AHI reduction. For moderate and severe OSA, objective testing should be undertaken because subjective report is not reliable
Guiding further adjustmentWhere symptoms have not fully resolved or the optimal setting is uncertain, objective data determine the therapeutic advancement
Persistent symptomsOngoing symptoms mean the appliance may not be in an adequate therapeutic position, or that adjustment or an alternative therapy is needed
Monitoring disease progres-sionSignificant weight change or new comorbidity can affect appliance therapy; objective testing re-evaluates efficacy after such changes
Complex or severe casesParticularly important in severe OSA, since even a reduced AHI remains associated with adverse health outcomes. Consider a mandibular advancement splint as a viable alternative to CPAP even in more severe OSA — but follow-up polysomnography is mandatory in these patients
Not indicatedPrimary snoring — follow-up polysomnography is not indicated unless symptoms worsen or fail to resolve

Maintenance of regular written communication with the patient's physician and other healthcare professionals concerning the treatment plan, progress and follow-up is recommended. The referring physician should be involved in ongoing evaluation, and the dentist should obtain a follow-up report on treatment efficacy from the sleep physician. In the case of unsatisfactory results, the dentist may extend the titration or review the treatment plan with the patient. Objective testing may also be used to compare the efficacy of a trial device with the final appliance, ensuring the optimal protrusion is achieved.

Clinical and instrumented titration

Clinical key point

Incremental titration aims for the most effective tolerable mandibular position. A symptom-guided trial and objective data may both inform adjustment; excessive protrusion is not automatically a stronger treatment.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 444 WORDS
TWOACCEPTED PAT H WAY S TO IDENTIFYING THE THE RAPEUTICPOSITION
Signs and symptoms onlyMultimethod positioning
1. Initial mandibular positioning 2. Resolution or improvement of signs and symptoms 3. Verification of treatment ef-ficacy by the physician1. Initial mandibular positioning 2. Resolution or improvement of signs and symptoms 3. Pulse oximetry, high-resolution pulse oximetry or HSAT administered by the qualified dentist, and/or HSAT or PSG ordered by the physician 4. Verification of treatment efficacy by the physician

Both pathways end at the same place. The dentist's objective data — where lawfully obtained — supplement the titration decision; they never replace physician verification. Advancement should continue until target parameter values are attained, can no longer be improved, or the maximum comfortable position is reached.

The process, step by step

Initial titration by the dentist. A qualified dentist selects, fits and titrates the appliance, adjusting the mandibular position to find the optimal level of protrusion.

Follow-up sleep study. The patient undergoes a follow-up study — home sleep test or attended polysomnography — while wearing the appliance at the dentist-determined setting. A home sleep test is a common method for confirming efficacy after initial fitting and titration; attended PSG is more comprehensive and can be used for both titration and final assessment.

Efficacy evaluation. The sleep physician analyses the results to determine effectiveness, using the AHI to assess severity, with a reduced AHI indicating successful treatment.

Further titration if needed. If the follow-up study shows an inadequately reduced AHI, further titration or adjustment is required. The follow-up study also identifies new or persistent adverse effects that may necessitate ad‐ justment.

Where the calibrated position is subtherapeutic, the medical provider and dentist discuss further calibration, adjunctive treatment or alternative treatment. In consultation with the physician a patient may elect to continue despite subtherapeutic results.

Advanced titration methods

Remotely controlled mandibular positioner (RCMP)— allows objective, single-night titration during polysom‐ nography to identify the optimal therapeutic advancement. This is the best-performing predictive method available, rated "excellent" against clinical and craniofacial prediction ("poor") and nasopharyngoscopy ("fair to good").

Drug-induced sleep endoscopy— a remotely controlled positioner can be used with DISE to titrate mandibular position under direct visualisation.

Note the honest limitation: demographic, anthropometric and polysomnographic data only weakly inform mandibu‐ lar advancement splint efficacy, which is precisely why trial-based methods outperform prediction from patient characteristics.

Further titration, modification, redesign or remake of the appliance should be undertaken as required. Patients should be monitored monthly for the first six months by the dentist and at least annually thereafter for as long as the appliance is worn.

Efficacy and effectiveness

Clinical key point

Distinguish complete disease control from a partial AHI response and from a meaningful but incomplete patient benefit. Explain residual AHI, oxygenation and symptoms separately rather than relying on a single “success percentage.”

EXAM TRAP — WHYEFFICACYFIGURES VA RY SOMUCH

The published figures range from 37% to 95% "success", and candidates are sometimes caught out by quoting a single number. The variation arises almost entirely from the definition used: cure (AHI <5), near-complete response (AHI <10 with ≥50% reduction), partial response (≥50% reduction), or symptomatic improvement. Always state the definition alongside the number — "approximately 64% achieve a 50% or greater reduction in AHI, but only about 37% reach an AHI below 5" — and the answer immediately reads as informed rather than recited.

Long-term effectiveness

Growing evidence suggests health outcomes, at least in the short term, are comparable between mandibular ad‐ vancement splints and CPAP, even in severe OSA, with greater adherence to the splint. Oral appliances also show beneficial effects on blood pressure, endothelial function and left ventricular function.

Long-term efficacy is good to about 10 years, and some studies show sustained effectiveness for up to five years.

However, real-world effectiveness declines over time, because some patients discontinue treatment on account of poor subjective effect or side effects, continuing patients may have a poorer objective effect, and the natural history of the disease is deterioration in AHI with age and weight gain. Effectiveness is also vulnerable to changes in the patient's bite and age, requiring continuous re-evaluation.

Limitations

Not as effective as CPAP in reducing sleep apnoea, snoring and improving daytime function — but with a definite role in the treatment of snoring and sleep apnoea

Adherence measurement

Clinical key point

Distinguish self-reported nightly wear from objective adherence sensors when available. Assessment of efficacy requires consideration of actual hours and nights of appliance use.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 2,301 WORDS
DEFINITION

AADSM definition of compliance: the appliance being worn for a minimum of≥80% per night— starting when the ap‐ pliance is placed in the mouth and ending when it is removed — for≥5 nights per week. Compliance discussions should stress that the patient should sleep a minimum of 7 hours per night and wear the appliance for the duration of their sleep every night. Contrast the CPAP research convention of≥4 hours per night.

Compliance monitoring technology

Objective monitoring uses embedded micro-sensors that measure temperature and wear time; some devices additionally track head position during sleep, and the most current appliances can also incorporate oximetry. Systems such as compliance-recording chips allow continuous and remote upload of data to a secure cloud, permitting real-time monitoring by clinicians. Objective measurement with an embedded micro-sensor thermometer is safe and feasible. Challenges for micro-sensors include saliva ingress, power supply, battery toxicity, fitting within the appliance without additional discomfort, compatibility with the appliance material, and verifiability.

Why it matters

It distinguishes device inefficacy from poor compliance— the single most important diagnostic function of adherence data. Without it you cannot know whether to advance the appliance or address behaviour.

It permits optimised titration and better treatment outcomes.

It supports legal and insurance documentation where proof of compliance is mandatory — notably in safety-sensit‐ ive occupations such as transport workers.

It allows patients to be categorised as fully compliant, improving over time, or non-compliant. Because patients with untreated or subtherapeutic OSA can eventually experience long-term health risks, non-compliant patients should be referred to their medical provider to consider alternative therapies.

Follow-up and reassessment in the first year

The steps involved in providing an appliance for a patient with OSA are conventionally set out as follows.

Insertion, adjustments, titration and evaluation during the first six months of regular use. The dentist monit‐ ors and collects information on the resolution of the patient's sleep disorder symptoms to assess efficacy and determine optimal titration. Where objective data are obtained with a portable monitor, they are used for titration only, and the dentist must be able to interpret the results accurately.

Referral of the patient for follow-up evaluation by the referring physician, with polysomnography or sleep studies required to verify treatment efficacy and benefit — because appliances may worsen OSA in some patients and placebo effects are known. Follow-up polysomnography is not indicated for patients with primary snoring unless symptoms worsen or do not resolve.

Maintenance of regular written communication with the physician and other professionals concerning the treatment plan, progress and follow-up. The referring physician should be involved in ongoing evaluation, and the dentist should obtain a follow-up report on treatment efficacy from the sleep physician. In the case of unsatisfactory results, the dentist may extend titration or review the treatment plan.

Further titration, modification, redesign or remake of the appliance as required. Patients should be monitored monthly for the first six months by the dentist and at least annually thereafter.

Follow-up and reassessment after six months to assess treatment adherence and efficacy. The dentist may advance or titrate further if symptoms recur. If maximum mandibular advancement is reached and symptoms are still present, the patient should be referred to the referring physician, who may then refer to a sleep specialist for further evaluation. The dentist should assess the integrity of the appliance, which may need replacement depending on usage and hygiene; appliances generally last approximately 2–3 years. For long-term efficacy, patients should be referred to their referring physician every five years for continuing assessment, as OSA is known to worsen with age and weight gain.

SELF-TEST — TOPIC 6

1. Describe a titration protocol from delivery to therapeutic position.

Set the appliance initially at 50% of maximum protrusion. Allow a two-week acclimatisation period at that setting. Then allow patient-led adjustment over approximately six weeks, advancing by one to two increments of 0.2–0.4 mm per night where symptoms persist — snoring, sleepiness, witnessed apnoeas. Hold the vertical dimension constant throughout. Continue until symptoms are adequately improved or further protrusion causes discomfort, remembering that symptoms improve before the AHI does and that overprotrusion may worsen the breathing. Review within 30 days of insertion, using the same question‐ naires as at baseline so change is quantifiable, and monthly for the first six months. Total titration usually takes two to five months — longer than CPAP, because the TMJ needs time to accustom to the advanced position. Once you judge titration adequate, arrange objective assessment, then refer to the physician for verification of efficacy.

2. Your patient reports complete resolution of snoring and feels much better. Is the treatment successful?

Not yet established. Subjective feedback alone is insufficient: oral appliances can worsen OSA in some patients, and there are known placebo effects with this therapy. Complete response requires symptom resolution and a treatment AHI below 5. I would arrange objective assessment — pulse oximetry or a home sleep test for titration purposes if lawful in my jurisdiction — and refer the patient back to the sleep physician for verification of treatment efficacy with polysomnography or an attended cardiorespiratory study, with the appliance in place at the final setting. The exception is primary snoring, for which follow-up sleep testing is not indicated unless symptoms worsen or fail to resolve.

3. You obtain a home sleep test showing residual AHI of 12 at maximum comfortable protrusion. What next?

First, confirm the data are being used appropriately — my objective data inform titration, and the physician verifies efficacy. Second, check whether the position is genuinely maximal and comfortable, and whether adherence is adequate; a residual AHI in a poorly adherent patient is a compliance problem, not a titration problem, which is exactly why objective adherence data matter. Third, if maximum mandibular advancement has been reached and symptoms persist, refer back to the referring physician, who may refer on to a sleep specialist. Options then include combination therapy — appliance plus positional therapy, plus nasal EPAP, or appliance plus CPAP at a lower pressure — weight management, treatment of nasal obstruction, or a switch of modality. I would document that a patient may, in consultation with the physician, elect to continue despite a subtherapeutic result, and that an AHI of 12 with resolved symptoms carries a relatively small residual risk — but that decision belongs to the patient and physician jointly, not to me alone.

4. Give the AADSM definition of compliance and contrast it with the CPAP convention.

The AADSM defines compliance as the appliance being worn for at least 80% of the night — timed from placement in the mouth to removal — on at least five nights per week, with the patient advised to sleep a minimum of seven hours nightly and wear the appliance for the whole duration of sleep. The CPAP research convention is much weaker: at least four hours per night. This matters when comparing modalities, because by the CPAP standard around 92% of appliance users would be classed compliant. Objective appliance adherence is approximately 80% at one month falling to 67% at six months and then stabilising, with mean use of 6.1–6.7 hours per night — exceeding typical CPAP use of about 5.2 hours.

5. Why is objective compliance monitoring important, beyond simply confirming use?

Its most important function is to distinguish device inefficacy from poor compliance— without that distinction you cannot know whether to advance the appliance or to address behaviour, and you risk over-protruding a patient who simply is not wearing the device. It also permits optimised titration and better outcomes; supports legal and insurance documentation where proof of compliance is mandatory, notably in safety-critical transport occupations; and allows categorisation as fully compliant, improving, or non-compliant — with non-compliant patients referred back to the medical provider to consider alternatives, since untreated or subtherapeutic OSA carries long-term health risk. Technology uses embedded micro-sensors recording temperature and wear time, sometimes with head position and oximetry, uploading remotely; the practical challenges are saliva, power supply, battery toxicity, bulk, material compatibility and verifiability.

6. A patient's snoring has resolved but their partner has recently died. What is the risk?

Discontinuation. Patients frequently stop wearing an appliance when the bed partner is no longer present or no longer complains of snoring, because in most cases the appliance is worn for snoring: the benefit is perceived by the bed partner while the side effects are experienced by the wearer. Once the perceived benefit disappears, only the cost remains. The management is to re-establish the patient's own reasons for treatment — daytime symptoms, blood pressure, cardiovascular and cognitive risk — to review objective adherence data, and to be alert to bereavement affecting both sleep and motivation more generally.
