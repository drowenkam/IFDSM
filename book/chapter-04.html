LEARNING OUTCOME §4.1

History, objectives and examination

Clinical key point

Record the chief complaint, medical diagnosis, sleep-study summary, symptom burden, prior therapy, expectations and patient priorities. A dental sleep examination is broader than a mandibular protrusion measurement.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 722 WORDS

Reason for attendance

Patients present with snoring, suspected or diagnosed OSA, sleep bruxism, or orofacial pain related to sleep disorders. Ask about sleep-related symptoms, previous diagnoses, comorbidities and the impact of sleep problems on daily life and well-being. Screening for OSA and related conditions is now recommended as part of routine dental care given the high prevalence and health risks of undiagnosed disease.

Goal setting

Collaborative goal setting means discussing the patient's primary concern — reducing snoring, improving sleep quality, alleviating pain — and aligning it with evidence-based options, explaining the benefits and limitations of appliance therapy and other interventions in the light of individual anatomical, behavioural and medical factors. In older adults or those with comorbidity, the goal may reasonably be improved quality of life and minimised side effects rather than normalisation of the AHI. Critically, before treatment the dentist should work with the medical provider and patient to determine the goal of treatment success and agree the metrics by which it will be judged, including how efficacy will be verified.

Managing expectations

Results vary, and some side effects — dental changes, discomfort — may occur.

Ongoing follow-up and adjustment are usually necessary.

Emphasise interdisciplinary care and, where required, referral to a sleep physician for comprehensive management.

Explain what additional therapies might be considered if the response to an appliance is inadequate.

Discuss sleep hygiene and duration — ambient lighting and temperature, electronics in bed, regular schedules. These may not directly affect appliance efficacy, but collectively they fragment sleep and exacerbate daytime sleepi‐ ness, reducing the patient's perception of benefit. A patient who sleeps five hours will not feel well however good the appliance.

EXAM TRAP — IMAGING

The default imaging for a dental sleep medicine work-up is panoramic and lateral cephalometric radiography as a pre- treatment record. CBCT is not routinely indicated. Candidates who advocate routine CBCT airway analysis lose marks on two grounds: unjustified radiation dose, and the poor validity of static airway measurement (see Topic 8 — the airway is dynamic, inter-examiner reliability is poor, normative values have wide standard deviations, and the commonest sites of collapse are precisely those least well visualised). Cephalometric evaluation is not always required for a patient who will use an oral appliance, but where deemed necessary it should be performed by appropriately trained professionals.

Treatment planning for OSA — what to cover

Provide explanation and written information on sleep behavioural therapy: sleep hygiene, sleeping position, weight control, alcohol intake.

Explain the type of device being considered and all alternatives— CPAP, surgery, positional therapy.

Propose various appliances according to the patient's oral health status and craniofacial morphology (mandibular advancement splint, tongue retaining device or equivalent).

Provide positional therapy information for patients with positional OSA.

Medical and lifestyle modifiers

Clinical key point

Assess weight, alcohol use, tobacco, nasal obstruction, current medications and sleep schedule, because these can affect OSA control and the probability of using an appliance consistently.

Other sleep disorders

Clinical key point

Ask about insomnia, restless legs, parasomnias and hypersomnolence disorders. Persistent fatigue after treatment does not by itself establish OAT failure or successful OSA control.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 503 WORDS
Coexisting conditionInteraction with SDB
Insomnia — COMISAInsomnia is highly prevalent in SDB, giving rise to the term comorbid insomnia and sleep apnoea. The overlap produces more severe sleep fragmentation, increased daytime sleepiness and poorer quality of life. Both conditions must be treated — reducing the AHI alone will not help the patient. CBT-I plus SDB treatment is the correct answer
Restless legs syndrome and PLMDFrequently co-occur with SDB. SDB can exacerbate limb movements and vice versa, causing further sleep disruption and increased arousals; both contribute to excessive daytime sleepiness and impaired alertness. Note that some antidepressants induce or worsen RLS and periodic limb movements
NarcolepsySDB may coexist with narcolepsy, compounding sleepiness and cognitive impairment. Symptom overlap makes clinical differentiation unreliable, so careful sleep studies are needed. Remember that SOREMPs on the MSLT — a narcolepsy criterion — are also frequent in untreated OSA
Insufficient sleep and shift work disorderSDB worsens the effects of both by further disrupting sleep architecture, increasing cognitive and cardiovascular risk. Shift workers with SDB are at higher risk of excessive sleepiness and accidents. A patient who is simply not spending enough time in bed will not respond to appliance therapy as expected
Mood and psychiatric disordersSDB is associated with mood disorders, depression and anxiety, which may themselves present with insomnia or hypersomnia, creating a cycle of worsening sleep and mental health

OSA and depression — a nuanced relationship worth knowing in detail

Symptoms of OSA and depression overlap— tiredness, fatigue, sleepiness, poor motivation and poor concentration — complicating the diagnosis and treatment of each. OSA may be responsible for antidepressant treatment failure, and depression is a risk factor for an incomplete response to CPAP, so it may need to be independently addressed.

Psychiatric diagnoses in OSA patients include depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder, psychotic disorders and bipolar disorder. Prevalence of depression is higher in OSA (~21.8%). Clinicians should evaluate OSA patients for depression initially by screening tool, and use established criteria to confirm the diagnosis if positive.

The relationship appears mutual rather than cause-and-effect. The pathophysiological relationship is not fully understood: it may be coincidental, or due to sleep fragmentation or repeated hypoxia, and confounders such as obesity, hypertension, diabetes and cardiovascular disease relate to both — insulin resistance, for example, contributes to depression as it does in OSA.

Proposed mechanisms: (i) sleep fragmentation and hypoxia of OSA as a cause of depression; (ii) the role of sero‐ tonin in both the neurobiology of depression and upper airway control — abnormalities in central and peripheral serotonergic neurotransmission are a potential factor in depression, and serotonin also affects upper airway dilator motor neurons via the hypoglossal nucleus, an effect further reduced in the sleep state; (iii) increased pro- inflammatory substances and cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF, which are responsible for daytime sleepiness.

The effect of CPAP on depression is not conclusive— current evidence that treating OSA improves depression is limited; some studies report a positive impact on both sleepiness and depression, others no effect.

Medical comorbidities

Clinical key point

Cardiac and pulmonary disease, stroke, neuromuscular weakness, opioid exposure, pregnancy or major medical change may alter testing and care pathways. Coordinate with the patient’s medical providers.

Ethics and patient consent

Clinical key point

Consent starts during planning: explain uncertainty of response, possible dental or bite changes, alternative treatments and the need for medical outcome verification.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 101 WORDS

The substance of this section is developed in Topics 9.4 and 10.1, which should be read together with it. In summary, the four classical principles apply — autonomy, non-maleficence, beneficence and justice— with veracity as a fifth, and the specific dental sleep medicine considerations are informed consent, scope of competence, collaboration and referral, the limits of the dentist's role in screening versus diagnosis, patient-centred care, confidentiality and quality assurance. Informed consent must be obtained in writing, before fitting the appliance, and must record the potential and probable side effects and the appliance's expected longevity.

Multidisciplinary communication

Clinical key point

Document diagnosis source, treatment plan, shared decisions and written communication between dentist, medical provider and general dentist when relevant. Define who is responsible for follow-up testing.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 1,237 WORDS
THE FOURGENERALPRINCIPLES — COMMIT THE S E TO MEMO RY

The diagnosis of OSA should always be made by a physician.

Oral appliances should be fitted by a qualified dentist who is trained and experienced in dental sleep medicine.

Follow-up assessment by the referring physician, with polysomnography or sleep studies, is required to verify treatment efficacy.

The dentist's role is to screen and provide the appliance, not to diagnose SDB.

Figure 4.2 — Portable monitoring decision tree. Flow chart depicting the recommended pathway for patients considered for portable monitoring: patients appropriate for PM should be at moderate-to-high risk for OSA, have no comorbid medical conditions and no comorbid sleep disorders. Patients not considered appropriate for PM should have in-laboratory polysomnography, and PM should be conducted under the supervision of a board-certified sleep specialist or an individual who fulfils the eligibility criteria for the sleep medicine certification examination.

Why it is examinable: this figure defines the boundary of the dentist's involvement with objective testing. Where a dentist may lawfully order or administer a home sleep apnoea test (jurisdiction-dependent), the resulting data are used for titration purposes only— never for diagnosis or formal follow-up assessment. Where diagnosis lies — the borderline cases

Patient presents with primary snoring without sleepiness→ still send to a sleep physician to exclude OSA before providing appliance therapy.

Patient has daytime sleepiness and suspected OSA→ refer.

Patient has sleep bruxism and orofacial pain or TMD→ these may be diagnosed by the dentist. But if accompan‐ ied by sleepiness, snoring or sleep apnoea, a sleep physician is required for diagnosis, and the patient should be advised to consult a professional trained in those fields.

Level 3 and 4 portable monitor results should be interpreted by a physician. The dentist mainly assesses the result and observes change in respiratory sleep parameters with treatment; the dentist does not use these data for diagnosis or as a formal final follow-up assessment. (Level 3: four or more channels including airflow — at least two channels of respiratory movement, or respiratory movement and airflow — heart rate or ECG and oxygen saturation. Level 4: one or two bioparameters, oxygen saturation or airflow.)

Code of practice and the staged treatment sequence

The dentist should complete board certification requirements for dental sleep medicine or obtain an equivalent diploma, and maintain a file containing patient history and examination results (which may include assessment of depression, mood and sleepiness), plus pre-treatment panoramic and cephalometric radiographs and dental casts.

Obtain a copy of the sleep record or polysomnography analysis, together with the OSA diagnosis, the physician's recommendation and an assessment of concomitant conditions from the sleep physician.

Perform clinical assessment — general and oral health, and the prognosis of the soft and hard tissues that will be affected by appliance therapy — and assess recent radiographs.

Obtain written consent, including potential risks, before fitting the appliance.

Maintain regular written communication with the physician about the treatment plan, progress and follow-up.

After starting therapy, collect information on the resolution of the patient's symptoms to determine optimal titration. If objective portable monitor data can be obtained, use them for titration purposes only.

Monitor symptom progress and obtain a follow-up report on treatment efficacy from the sleep physician. If unsatisfactory, extend titration or review the treatment plan with the patient. Primary snoring requires no objective follow-up assessment.

Establish a follow-up protocol after physician assessment: annual follow-up assessing efficacy, compliance, side effects and the integrity of the oral structures; the appliance may need replacement depending on usage and hygiene.

Ensure the patient attends control visits, and keep the communication between physician and dentist on file. If an additional titration period is needed, consult the medical team.

EXAM TRAP — THE SINGLECOMMONEST WAY TO LOSEMARKSINTHISEXAMIN AT I O N

Any answer that implies the dentist diagnoses OSA, or that treatment may begin on the strength of the dentist's own screen‐ ing, is wrong — regardless of how clinically sensible the rest of the answer is. Equally, any answer that has the dentist certi‐ fying treatment success from their own portable monitor data is wrong. The correct formulation is: "I screen, refer, select, fit, calibrate, monitor and manage side effects; the physician diagnoses and verifies efficacy; and where I use oximetry or a home test it is to identify the therapeutic position, for titration purposes only."

The dentist's screening role, in the words of the guidance

Dentists screen for potential patients with SDB or OSA using questionnaires (STOP-BANG, Epworth Sleepiness Scale, Friedman Tongue Classification, Kushida Index, Berlin Questionnaire), evaluation of neck size and BMI, and the modified Mallampati classification to describe the patency of the oral airway; then refer patients suspected of SDB or OSA to a sleep physician or otolaryngologist; and then apply, manage and follow up the appliance.

LEARNING OUTCOME §4.7

Sleep bruxism

Clinical key point

Sleep bruxism may coexist with OSA but does not diagnose it. A protective splint is not an OSA treatment; consider respiratory symptoms before fitting a device that changes mandibular position.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 1,294 WORDS
DEFINITIONS TO KNOW VERBATIM

Sleep bruxism— a masticatory muscle activity during sleep, characterised as rhythmic (phasic — grinding) or non- rhythmic (tonic — clenching), and not a movement disorder or a sleep disorder in otherwise healthy individuals.

The critical corollary: sleep bruxism is not a disorder but a behaviour that may be a risk factor or a protective factor for certain clinical consequences. In terms of clinical consequences it may be classified as: not a risk or protective factor (a harmless behaviour); a risk factor (related to one or more negative health outcomes); or a protective factor (related to one or more positive health outcomes). It is also classified as primary or secondary.

Temporomandibular disorder (TMD)— a musculoskeletal and neuromuscular condition involving clinical signs and symptoms of the muscles of mastication, the temporomandibular joint and related structures.

Assessment grading (international consensus): possible (self-report), probable (self-report plus clinical examination), and definite (self-report, clinical examination and instrumental assessment, notably polysomnography with EMG). Standard cut- off points should not be applied in otherwise healthy individuals; bruxism-related masticatory muscle activity should be assessed along a continuum of the behaviour.

Prevalence

Wide reported range: 6–91%, depending on assessment method.

Self-reported sleep bruxism: ~8% of the adult population.

Children and adolescents: 14–46%.

Up to 30–50% of adults with OSA also exhibit sleep bruxism.

Prevalence of TMD in OSA populations: 2–52%. Prevalence of OSA in TMD populations: ~28%.

Pathophysiology of sleep bruxism

Macro-sleep structure: episodes occur more commonly in stages N2 to N3, in a cyclical pattern, and are typically associated with micro-arousals

Stress: more clearly linked to awake bruxism and anxiety

Pain: paradoxically, TMD may reduce bruxism

Neurochemistry: catecholaminergic mechanisms, particularly described in children

Airway

Autonomic cardiac activation

Genetics

Swallowing: bruxism may increase salivary flow to neutralise acid from gastro-oesophageal reflux

The relationship with SDB — how to phrase it

Figure 4.3 — Evidence strength for three claims about sleep bruxism and SDB."SB and SDB frequently co-occur" — strong, be‐ cause multiple studies show high comorbidity. "SDB directly causes SB" — weak, because the evidence is inconsistent and often lacking. "Treating SDB can reduce SB (if temporally linked)" — moderate, supported by some case and intervention studies.

Why it is examinable: memorise these three gradings. They give you a defensible, precisely calibrated answer to a question where most candidates either overclaim causation or dismiss the association altogether.

Common clinical features shared by SB and SDB: both are commoner in supine sleep; both involve oropharyngeal and masticatory muscle activation; both relate to sleep arousals (micro-arousals in SB, major arousals in OSA); both are associated with GORD, TMD and headache; both may respond to CPAP (case reports of CPAP treating SB), to UPPP (which can treat SB in children with enlarged tonsils), and to mandibular advancement (which decreases SB in the short term). Shared risk factors include age, obesity, smoking and alcohol use.

The "reactive–protective" hypothesis, and its limits

The proposed sequence is: muscle tone falls during sleep, leading to obstruction → more negative intraluminal pressure → bruxism is triggered, increasing muscle tone and restoring airway patency → less negative pressure. Supporting observations: muscle tone is inversely related to upper airway obstruction; on polysomnography a sudden peak of respiratory effort can be seen together with chin muscle activity, followed by masseter activity and EEG evidence of

arousal; some OSA treatments have a positive effect on SB and perhaps on TMD; and occlusal splints (e.g. Michigan- type) aggravate SDB, with increased AHI and snoring.

Against it: it is not known whether this is a true reactive–protective response or simply a non-specific arousal response — the same arousal produces other motor activity such as leg movement. Meta-analyses and systematic reviews indicate that, overall, no significant or consistent correlation exists between SB and OSA across all populations and severities. Gold-standard polysomnographic studies have found no consistent temporal association, and more SB events have been shown to precede apnoea–hypopnoea events than follow them — which, if SB occurred after the event, would argue against a protective role. The relationship is likely multifactorial, involving shared arousal mechanisms rather than causality, and may be stronger in certain phenotypes such as body-position-related OSA. Other findings do suggest a real subgroup effect: a subpopulation of young SB patients shows concomitant respiratory events; a phenotypic subtype of OSA patients had SB with significantly more respiratory-related arousals and desaturation; SB has a higher prevalence in OSA patients; AHI and desaturation events are more frequent in SB patients; and grinding frequency correlates with apnoea, micro-arousal and desaturation.

HIGH-YIELD — THE SENTENCE TO S AY

"Sleep bruxism and OSA frequently co-occur and share plausible mechanisms — chiefly sleep arousal and autonomic activation — but a direct causal relationship is not established. The dentist should refrain from implying a causal rela‐ tionship when discussing these conditions with patients. Treating the OSA can reduce sleep bruxism in some patients, particularly where the bruxism is temporally linked to apnoeic events."

TMD and appliance therapyTMD is not a contraindication to a MAD. Transient jaw discomfort does not necessarily indicate TMD; only around 2% cannot tolerate the appliance. Mandibular posture with a MAD produces no significant TMJ morphological alteration, and condylar translation with a MAD is less than that in the maximum open position. Long-term MAD use does not impair the joint — TMD symptom intensity may reduce, with reduction of joint noise and jaw fatigue, and without jaw stiffness or pain. MADs do produce more pain-related TMD than CPAP, but this is transient and non-serious. TMJ disc displacement is not a contraindic-ation. The onset of arthrogenous and myogenous pain is not related to pre-existing TMD, and patients with pre-existing TMD do not generally experience exacerbation — but mod-erate-to-severe TMD should be treated before starting appliance therapy to reduce the drop-out rate. Compression-type devices produce greater early masticatory myalgia and residual tongue pain than traction devices, which impose lower stress
Managing TMD in a patient on OATManage as a musculoskeletal disorder, informed by microtrauma principles and formal diagnostic criteria. Mandibular exercises allow patients with TMD to wear a MAD — improving sleep and quality of life, reducing pain intensity and increasing compliance
EXAM TRAP — THE OCCLUSALSPLINT

A conventional flat-plane stabilisation (Michigan-type) splint can worsen sleep-disordered breathing: AHI and snoring both increase, because the increase in vertical dimension and posterior mandibular positioning reduce airway space. Therefore, before fitting any splint for bruxism, screen for OSA. If OSA is likely, a mandibular advancement device may serve both purposes. This is one of the most practically important pieces of knowledge in the whole syllabus and a very likely examination question.

LEARNING OUTCOME §4.8

Pain, TMD and sleep

Clinical key point

TMD is assessed clinically and managed proportionately. Pain or limited opening may constrain OAT design or titration, but a past TMD diagnosis does not always exclude OAT.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 2,420 WORDS

Pain and sleep have a bidirectional, cyclical relationship. Pain disrupts sleep; poor sleep worsens pain; and a bad night increases pain sensitivity, leading to more pain and further sleep disturbance.

How pain affects sleepHow sleep affects pain

• •

Prevents sleep onset and causes frequent awakenings Acute experimental painful stimuli induce arousal and wakefulness in healthy individuals Pain is a primary cause of insomnia; a high proportion of chronic pain patients have insomnia or other sleep difficulties Nociceptive stimuli produce transient EEG changes, shifts to lighter sleep stages and brief arousals

Hyperalgesia— short or disturbed sleep increases sensitivity to painful stimulation Spontaneous pain — sleep deficiency can cause or worsen muscle pain and headache Reduced pain tolerance, with increased fatigue and more severe subjective pain Reduced antinociceptive potency of morphine

Underlying mechanisms: dysregulated neurotransmitters — serotonin, noradrenaline and dopamine — involved in both pain and sleep regulation; pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and IL-6 involved in both pathways; and dysfunction of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, the central stress system, in individuals with chronic pain and sleep disorders.

Clinical implications: treatment of chronic pain should simultaneously address sleep, usually requiring a multidiscip‐ linary approach; psychological input is valuable for the mood and anxiety components; and improving sleep quality improves pain tolerance and overall quality of life.

Aetiology May appear for no apparent reason, or follow a viral illness, an emotionally distressing event, a work-related soft tissue injury, or a whiplash neck injury following a road traffic accident
Other features Patients often describe other pain problems — migraine or chronic headache, irritable bowel syndrome, atypical chest pain, irritable bladder syndrome and temporomandibular joint pain. Shared features and diagnosis Both are complex chronic illnesses of unknown aetiology characterised primarily by pain, fatigue and sleep disturbance, with considerable clinical overlap; both feature the perception of poor-quality, unrefreshing sleep. Patients with severe fatigue, sleep disturbance and pain should have a thorough history including medical and psychoso-cial circumstances and changes in sleep patterns around symptom onset, plus a careful history of prior illnesses, psychiatric disorders, alcohol or substance abuse, allergies, current prescription and over-the-counter medications and herbal supplements. There is no rational established approach to the treatment of pain-related sleep disturbance, and it is not clear that pain is actually the cause of disrupted sleep in chronic pain patients
EXAM TRAP — FIBROM YA LGIACRITERIA

The 11-of-18 tender point count is the 1990 ACR classification criteria. The ACR published revised criteria in 2010 (with a 2011 modification and a 2016 revision) which abandoned the tender point examination in favour of the Widespread Pain Index and Symptom Severity Scale. Your source notes use the 1990 criteria. Quote the tender point count if asked — it remains the classic examinable fact and is what most syllabi expect — but signal that you know the criteria have since been revised. That single sentence turns a correct answer into an impressive one.

SELF-TEST — TOPIC 4

1. List the imaging you would obtain before commencing oral appliance therapy, and justify what you would not obtain.

Panoramic and lateral cephalometric radiographs as a pre-treatment record, plus initial dental radiographs as indicated, together with intraoral and extraoral photographs and study casts or digital scans. CBCT and other CT are not indicated at this stage. The justification for declining CBCT is twofold: unjustified radiation exposure without a clear diagnostic yield, and the poor validity of static airway measurement — the airway is dynamic, inter-examiner reliability is poor, normative values have wide standard deviations, and the sites most often responsible for collapse are the least well visualised. Cephalometric evaluation is not always required, but where it is deemed necessary it should be performed by appropriately trained clinicians.

2. A patient is referred by their GP with loud snoring and an ESS of 14, requesting an appliance. There is no sleep study. What do you do?

I do not commence treatment. Snoring cannot be distinguished from OSA clinically, and treatment must not begin in the absence of a diagnosis made by a physician. I would perform my screening assessment — history, ESS, STOP-BANG, BMI, blood pressure, neck circumference, and the dental and craniofacial examination — and refer to a sleep physician for clinical review and diagnostic testing, sending my findings with the referral. I would also assess and document dental suitability so that treatment can start promptly once a diagnosis and prescription are received. Where local law permits a dentist to order a home sleep apnoea test, I could do so, but the patient must still be referred to a medical provider for the diagnosis.

3. Describe the shared care model, allocating each responsibility.

The physician diagnoses OSA — always — having seen the patient clinically, interprets the sleep study, prescribes appliance therapy in writing with the diagnostic report, verifies treatment efficacy with follow-up sleep testing, and remains involved in ongoing evaluation. The dentist screens for symptoms and signs, refers, assesses dental suitability, treats oral disease first, selects and fits an appropriate custom titratable appliance, calibrates it, provides behavioural advice, monitors adherence and the integrity of the oral structures, manages side effects, and refers on to periodontics, orthodontics or prosthodontics where needed. Both maintain regular written communication about the treatment plan, progress and follow-up, and a systematic follow-up programme is agreed between them. The dentist may obtain objective data by portable monitor for titration pur‐ poses only.

4. What is the relationship between sleep bruxism and OSA?

They frequently co-occur — up to 30–50% of adults with OSA also exhibit sleep bruxism — and share plausible mechanisms, principally sleep arousal and autonomic activation, plus common risk factors of age, obesity, smoking and alcohol. However, meta-analyses show no consistent correlation across populations and severities, gold-standard polysomnographic studies find no consistent temporal association, and more bruxism events have been shown to precede apnoea–hypopnoea events than follow them, arguing against a straightforward protective role. A causal relationship is therefore not established, and the dent‐ ist should refrain from implying causation when discussing these conditions with patients. Treating the OSA can reduce bruxism in some patients where the two are temporally linked. Graded by evidence strength: co-occurrence strong, causation weak, treatment effect moderate.

5. A patient requests a night guard for tooth wear. What must you establish first, and why?

Screen for OSA before fitting any splint. A conventional flat-plane stabilisation (Michigan-type) splint can worsen sleep-dis‐ ordered breathing, increasing both AHI and snoring, because of the increased vertical dimension and posterior mandibular position. If there are signs or symptoms of OSA, refer for diagnosis and consider a mandibular advancement device, which can address both the bruxism-related protection and the airway. Note also that sleep bruxism alone requires neither a sleep study nor mandibular advancement, and an occlusal splint is appropriate first-line therapy in that situation.

6. Is temporomandibular disorder a contraindication to oral appliance therapy?

Not absolutely. TMD is a relative contraindication: active severe TMJ disease or pain should be treated before starting therapy, principally to reduce the drop-out rate, but disc displacement alone is not a contraindication and pre-existing TMD does not generally worsen with appliance use. Transient jaw discomfort is common and does not itself indicate TMD; only around 2% of patients cannot tolerate an appliance. Long-term use does not impair the joint — condylar translation is less than in maximum opening, there is no significant morphological alteration, and TMD symptom intensity, joint noise and jaw fatigue may actually reduce. Mandibular exercises allow patients with TMD to wear an appliance, improving sleep and quality of life, reducing pain intensity and increasing compliance. Traction-type devices impose lower stress than compression-type devices, which produce greater early masticatory myalgia and residual tongue pain.

7. Explain the bidirectional relationship between pain and sleep, with mechanisms.

Pain prevents sleep onset, causes frequent awakenings, produces transient EEG changes and shifts to lighter stages, and is a primary cause of insomnia. Conversely, short or disturbed sleep produces hyperalgesia — lowered pain thresholds and increased sensitivity — can cause or worsen spontaneous muscle pain and headache, reduces pain tolerance, and reduces the antinociceptive potency of morphine. The result is a self-reinforcing cycle. Mechanisms include dysregulation of serotonin, noradrenaline and dopamine, which regulate both pain and sleep; pro-inflammatory cytokines including TNF-α and IL-6 acting in both pathways; and HPA axis dysfunction. The clinical implication is that chronic pain management must address sleep simultaneously, usually through a multidisciplinary approach.
