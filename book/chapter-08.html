Paediatric prevalence

Clinical key point

Paediatric OSA occurs at lower respiratory-event indices than adult OSA. Prevalence estimates depend on age and scoring; do not import adult AHI thresholds into a child’s report.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 57 WORDS

OSA: 1–5%, with many sources suggesting around 2–3%.

Habitual snoring: 3–12% (some sources cite 10–20%).

Age and obesity influence prevalence, with peaks in younger children (2–8 years) and again in adolescents.

Prevalence varies by population, gender and race.

Most commonly affects children aged 3–6 years, when tonsillar tissue is proportionally largest.

Aetiology and pathophysiology

Clinical key point

Enlarged tonsils/adenoids, craniofacial restriction, obesity and neuromuscular factors may contribute. The dominant cause changes with age and comorbidity.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 474 WORDS

Aetiology

CauseDetail
Adenotonsillar hypertrophyThe most frequent cause of airway obstruction in children, particularly in pre-schoolers
ObesityA significant risk factor, especially in teenagers, contributing to airway narrowing
Craniofacial abnormalityMicrognathia, a narrowed midface, and other structural differences that reduce airway dimension and increase collapsibility
Allergic rhinitisChronic nasal congestion contributing to obstruction
Neuromuscular disordersDown syndrome, cerebral palsy — affecting head and neck muscle tone, so that muscles relax excessively during sleep leading to airway closure
Tumours or other growthsRarely, a growth in the airway causes physical blockage

Additional anatomical risk factors described across the literature: micrognathia, retrognathia, macroglossia, adenotonsillar hypertrophy, nasal septal deviation, and a high-arched or narrow hard palate. Note the important caveat: caution should be taken when using tonsil size evaluation, because tonsil size does not correlate with OSA sever‐ ity(Brodsky tonsil classification).

Pathophysiology — the sequence

Muscle relaxation. As the child falls asleep, the muscles that keep the upper airway open naturally relax.

Airway narrowing or collapse. Where the airway is already narrow — large tonsils, a small jaw — or where muscle tone is reduced, relaxation brings the airway tissues closer together or causes them to fold, producing partial or complete blockage.

Respiratory effort and arousal. During obstruction the brain detects falling oxygen and rising carbon dioxide, and triggers a brief awakening to restore muscle tone and reopen the airway.

Sleep disruption. These frequent arousals, often not remembered, fragment sleep and reduce restorative stages, producing daytime fatigue, hyperactivity and poor concentration.

Consequences. Over time this pattern affects growth, neurocognitive development, academic performance and emotional well-being.

The four contributing mechanisms in paediatric OSA are conventionally listed as craniofacial morphology, tonsillar hypertrophy, altered neuromuscular tone and ventilatory control.

Adenoid facies (long face syndrome)

An atypical orofacial growth pattern associated with chronic mouth breathing and nasal obstruction: a long face with increased lower facial height, a convex profile, mouth breathing with incompetent lip seal and open mouth posture, a high-arched palate, a constricted maxilla usually causing posterior crossbites and crowded teeth, a retruded mandible, and sometimes an anterior open bite. The consequences of a reduced upper airway are therefore not only sleep- breathing problems but also a constricted maxilla with posterior crossbite and crowding, together with impaired growth, cardiovascular problems, and learning and behavioural problems.

EXAM TRAP — THE MALOCCLUSION – OSALINKISWEAKERTHANDENTISTSASSUME

Most children with OSA do not have obviously altered craniofacial morphology; the measured differences are frequently not clinically relevant. Dentists overestimate the correlation between malocclusion and the prevalence of OSA. A great‐ er prevalence of malocclusion has not been found to be associated with OSA, and abnormal respiration or OSA is not a frequent or common cause of malocclusion. Adenoid facies is a recognisable pattern, not a reliable screening test. Stating this explicitly demonstrates critical reading; asserting a strong causal link between malocclusion and OSA does the opposite.

Insufficient sleep and development

Clinical key point

Insufficient sleep can mimic or amplify behavioural and cognitive complaints. Establish age-appropriate sleep opportunities and schedule, while assessing SDB when suggested by history.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 404 WORDS

Recommended sleep duration

AgeRecommended sleep per 24 hours
Infant <4 monthsNot specified — variation too large
Infant 4–12 months12–16 hours
Children 1–2 years11–14 hours
Children 3–5 years10–13 hours
Children 6–12 years9–12 hours
Teenagers 13–18 years8–10 hours

Less than recommended: attention, behaviour and learning problems; increased risk of accidents, injuries, hypertension, obesity, diabetes and depression; and in teenagers, increased risk of self-harm, suicidal thoughts and suicide attempts. Sufficient sleep: improved attention, behaviour, learning, memory, emotional regulation, quality of life, and mental and physical health.

LEARNING OUTCOME §8.5

Adolescent sleep and performance

Clinical key point

Adolescents commonly have a delayed circadian phase compounded by early school hours and device use. Short sleep does not prove OSA; assess timing, regularity and respiratory symptoms.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 657 WORDS
THE FRAMINGSENTENCE

The reduction of sleep in adolescents is not driven by a lower need for sleep, but by biological, psychological and socio- cultural influences acting together.

Biological processes

Circadian phase delay. Early adolescent development and pubertal onset are associated with a more evening-type circadian phase preference— the preferred timing of sleep, wake and daytime activity shifts later.

Sleep–wake homeostasis. Sleep pressure builds more slowly with time awake in adolescents. Together with the phase delay this makes it a major challenge for young people to fall asleep in the early evening and to wake refreshed in the early morning.

Weekend catch-up. Going to bed later and waking later at weekends reflects circadian biology and is also a response to insufficient weekday sleep — but later weekend sleep timing and catch-up sleep further delay the sig‐ nal for the biological night, perpetuating the cycle.

Electronic media

Television, music players, games consoles, computers and phones act through four mechanisms: media use directly dis‐ places sleep; devices allow greater interaction with friends at night; light from devices suppresses melatonin and dis‐ rupts circadian rhythm, producing inability to fall asleep at a reasonable time; and use increases sleep-disrupting mental, emotional and physiological arousal.

School start times

Early school start times conflict with the delayed circadian rhythm and produce chronic insufficient sleep. Later-starting middle schools report later rise times, more total sleep on school nights, less daytime sleepiness, less tardiness, fewer attention and concentration difficulties, better academic performance, and better safety with a reduced crash rate.

Caffeine

Shorter sleep duration, increased sleep onset latency, increased wake after sleep onset, and increased daytime sleepi‐ ness.

Consumers are twice as likely to have difficulty sleeping and to report morning sleepiness — establishing a vicious cycle in which sleepiness prompts more caffeine.

Caffeine reduces the percentage of time in slow-wave sleep in a dose-related manner and alters the temporal organ‐ isation of REM and NREM sleep, affecting learning and memory consolidation.

Females are more likely to show tolerance and withdrawal symptoms.

Associated with higher risk of other substance use and abuse and other risk-taking behaviour, and inversely related to academic achievement.

Other causes

Chronic medical illness

Over-the-counter or prescribed medication to induce sleep, leading to residual daytime sleepiness

Mental health problems — anxiety, stress

Alcohol consumption

Prescribed psychotropic medication

Academic demands: excessive homework and extracurricular load

Chronic respiratory illness such as asthma

Pain conditions such as migraine

Use of stimulants — especially those prescribed for ADHD — as a countermeasure to sleepiness or as academic performance enhancers

CognitionPoor judgement, lack of motivation, inattention and affective dysregulation. Higher-level cognitive executive functions, for which adolescence is a critical developmental period, are selectively af-fected by sleep loss — problem-solving and decision-making in particular
Mood and self-harmShorter school-night total sleep time is associated with daytime sleepiness and depressive symptoms. Sleeping less than 8 hours is associated with an almost threefold increased risk of suicide attempts. Increased risk of mood disorders and suicidal ideation
Risk-takingIrregular sleep patterns and self-reported sleep problems — rather than sleep loss per se — are associated with increased risk-taking behaviour. Heightened impulsivity and emotional dysregula-tion; impaired judgement increases risky choices by focusing on potential rewards rather than consequences. Paediatric screening

Clinical key point

A parental screening questionnaire or oral findings may identify high-risk children but cannot establish OSA severity. Prioritise appropriate paediatric medical assessment and testing.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 1,501 WORDS

History and general assessment

Medical, dental and family history: chronic allergies, asthma, recurrent ear or throat infections, and a family history of sleep apnoea or SDB. Obesity, prematurity and syndromic conditions increase risk.

Cognitive, behavioural and developmental assessment: children with SDB often display hyperactivity, irritability and difficulty concentrating, closely resembling ADHD.

Growth and physical development: assess for delayed growth or failure to thrive, which may signal disrupted sleep and insufficient oxygenation during growth. Dentists should assess height, weight and neck size to screen paediatric patients for OSA.

Screening tools

ToolStructure, threshold and role
Paediatric Sleep Question-naire (PSQ)The most widely used paediatric instrument, structured into four sub-scores: breathing, beha-viour, sleepiness and other domains. A total score exceeding 0.33 — meaning more than 8 of the 22 questions (33%) answered positively — suggests a high likelihood of SDB. Not diagnostic, but recognised as an effective initial screening tool and one of the most sensitive questionnaires available for detecting SDB
OSA-18Assesses quality of life and SDB symptoms; a complementary tool to the PSQ
Modified paediatric Ep-worth (ESS-CHAD)For children aged 6–16; evaluates daytime sleepiness and prompts further investigation if ab-normal
FAIREST-6Designed to identify red-flag indicators of paediatric SDB. Scoring: 2 = mild, 4 = moderate, 6 = severe increase in risk

Figure 8.2 — FAIREST-6: six red flags for paediatric sleep-disordered breathing.(1) Mouth breathing— difficulty with sustained nasal breathing for three minutes; (2) mentalis strain; (3) tonsil hypertrophy— <50% versus >50% obstruction, using the illustrated grading; (4) ankyloglossia— restricted versus not restricted tongue range of motion; (5) dental wear— visible signs of wear; (6) nar‐ row palate— signs of dental crowding, high arch, and/or narrow palate. Each item is scored independently as a red flag, with the grading scale converting the number of red flags to a risk level.

Why it is examinable: this is the most dentally-oriented paediatric screening tool in existence — every one of the six items is something a dentist observes routinely. Learn all six, and the 2/4/6 grading.

Figure 8.3 — Modified Mallampati grades (I–IV) and Brodsky tonsil grades (0–4). Mallampati: class 1 — soft palate, uvula, fauces and tonsillar pillars all visible; class 2 — soft palate and the majority of the uvula visible; class 3 — soft palate and the base of the uvula only; class 4 — only the hard palate visible. Brodsky: grade 0 — tonsils within the pillars; grade 1 — tonsils occupying <25% of the space between the anterior pillars; grade 2 — occupying 25–50%; grade 3 — occupying 50–75%; grade 4 — occupying >75% of the space between the anterior pillars.

Why it is examinable: two classifications frequently confused with one another. Mallampati grades oral airway patency by tongue posi‐ tion; Brodsky grades tonsil size by percentage of oropharyngeal width. Learn both scales precisely, and pair them with the caution that tonsil size does not correlate with OSA severity.

Figure 8.4 — Brodsky tonsil grading in detail: grade 1, tonsil within the pillars; grade 2, tonsil <50% from midpoint between uvula and pillar; grade 3, tonsil >50% crossing the midpoint between uvula and pillar; grade 4, tonsils meet in the midline.

Why it is examinable: the practical technique matters — depress the tongue and ask the patient to say "ah". Grade 3 or 4 is one of the "at risk anatomically" findings you should document (Topic 5), and is an exclusion criterion for hypoglossal nerve stimulation (Topic 3).

Figure 8.5 — Typical clinical presentation of a paediatric patient with mouth breathing: facial appearance with open mouth posture and incompetent lip seal, the intraoral appearance of the associated narrow, high-arched maxillary arch with crowding, and the corresponding radiographic appearance.

Why it is examinable: this is "adenoid facies" as it actually presents. Use it to anchor the constellation — long face, convex profile, mouth breathing, incompetent lip seal, high-arched constricted maxilla, posterior crossbite, crowding, retruded mandible — while remembering the caveat in 8.2 that most children with OSA do not show obvious morphological change.

Figure 8.6 — Supplementary guides: the functional classification of ankyloglossia based on the tongue range of motion ratio (TRMR), shown for both the tongue-tip (TRMR-TIP) and the lingual-palatal suction (TRMR-LPS) positions with graded photographic examples; and reference values for maxillary intermolar distance— adult measurements graded <32, 32–34, 34–36, 36–38 and 38–42 mm, with paediatric measurements for age +24 months.

Why it is examinable: these give you objective, defensible numbers for two findings dentists otherwise describe only subjectively — tongue restriction and arch width. Quoting a measured intermolar distance rather than "the arch looks narrow" is exactly the shift in rigour examiners reward.

Figure 8.7 — The Paediatric Sleep Questionnaire (PSQ), answered yes / no / don't know by the caregiver, covering three groups of items: while sleeping (snoring more than half the time, always snoring, loud snoring, heavy or loud breathing, trouble breathing or struggling to breathe), witnessed events (stopping breathing during the night), and daytime and behavioural items (mouth breathing during the day, dry mouth on waking, bed-wetting, waking unrefreshed, daytime sleepiness, teacher-reported daytime sleepiness, difficulty waking, morning headaches, poor growth, overweight), followed by attention and behaviour items (not listening, difficulty organising tasks, easy distraction, fidgeting, inability to remain seated, interrupting others).

Why it is examinable: note how many items are directly observable by a dentist or reported by a parent in a dental chair, and note the substantial behavioural section — the questionnaire's structure embodies the central paediatric teaching point that children present with behaviour rather than sleepiness.

Figure 8.8 — The OSA-18 quality-of-life survey for the evaluation of sleep-disordered breathing, with its five domains: sleep disturb‐ ance(loud snoring, breath-holding or struggling to breathe, choking or gasping, restless sleep or frequent awakening), physical suffer‐ ing(mouth breathing from nasal obstruction, frequent colds or upper respiratory infections, nasal discharge or runny nose, difficulty swallowing food), emotional distress(mood swings or temper tantrums, aggressive or hyperactive behaviour, discipline problems), day‐ time problems(excessive daytime drowsiness or sleepiness, poor attention span or concentration, difficulty getting out of bed in the morning) and caregiver concerns(worry over the child's general health, concern that the child is not getting enough air, interference with the caregiver's ability to perform daily activities, frustration), plus a global quality-of-life rating scale.

Why it is examinable: the OSA-18 is the standard measure of the burden of paediatric SDB on the child and family, complementary to the PSQ's symptom focus. Its inclusion of a caregiver-concerns domain is a reminder that paediatric SDB is a family diagnosis.

Figure 8.9 — The paediatric Epworth Sleepiness Scale, rating the likelihood of the child dozing off in eight situations — sitting and reading, watching television, sitting inactive in a public place, being a passenger in a motor vehicle for an hour without a break, lying down in the afternoon when circumstances permit, sitting and talking to someone, sitting quietly after lunch, and doing homework or taking a test — each scored 0 to 3. A total score of 10 or more indicates that the child is sleepy.

Why it is examinable: note the adaptation of the adult instrument — "doing homework or taking a test" replaces the adult "in a car, stopped in traffic". The same caveat applies as in adults: it measures sleep propensity and cannot diagnose OSA, and children with significant OSA frequently score normally because they present with hyperactivity rather than sleepiness.

Treatment approaches

Clinical key point

Adenotonsillectomy is frequently first-line when adenotonsillar hypertrophy contributes. Some children have persistent disease and need further medical and sometimes orthodontic, weight-related or PAP approaches.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 1,063 WORDS

Adenotonsillectomy — first line

AspectDetail
StatusFirst-line treatment and the most effective surgical therapy for paediatric patients, improving poly-somnographic parameters, behaviour and quality of life significantly
LimitationsPost-operative persistence of OSA after adenotonsillectomy is common. Adenotonsillectomy alone may be insufficient because it does not address oropharyngeal obstruction secondary to tonsillar hyperplasia, especially in children with underlying illness — Down syndrome, craniofacial anomalies, neuromuscular disease
Risk factors for treat-ment failureObesity; baseline severity (high pre-operative AHI); age (<3 years or ≥7 years have a higher failure rate); nasal abnormality or craniofacial disproportion; family history of OSA; asthma. Post-operat-ive polysomnography is suggested for these high-risk patients even in the absence of persistent snoring — although persistent OSA is less likely if there is no snoring post-operatively
ContraindicationSubmucous cleft palate is a relative contraindication — a partial adenotonsillectomy may be performed instead
Post-operative manage-mentUsually performed as an outpatient. For patients at high risk of post-operative complications, consider an overnight hospital stay — specifically where the SpO2 nadir is <80%, the AHI ≥24/h, or the peak PCO2 ≥60 mmHg (hypercapnia), because of the increased risk of post-operative respiratory compromise
Risk factors for post-op-erative complicationsAge <3 years; severe OSA on polysomnography; cardiac complications; failure to thrive; obesity; upper respiratory tract infection — if acute URI on the day of surgery, reschedule or monitor closely post-operatively, but be aware that children with adenotonsillar hypertrophy exhibit chronic rhinorrhoea and nasal congestion even without viral infection; other medical comorbidity
Partial tonsillectomyA portion of tonsil tissue is left covering the musculature of the tonsillar fossa. Advantages: re-duced morbidity, less immediate post-operative pain, earlier return to normal activity and diet. Dis‐
AspectDetail
advantages: increased risk of tonsil regrowth (0.5–16%), increased intra-operative blood loss, risk of recurrent infection in the tonsillar remnant, and the need for long-term monitoring for OSA symptoms related to regrowth and recurrence
Other surgical optionsTurbinate reduction, septoplasty; expansion sphincter pharyngoplasty for refractory cases; craniofacial procedures such as mandibular distraction osteogenesis for children with craniofacial anomalies; tracheotomy as a last resort when all other treatment fails — very effective but with much morbidity

CPAP

Effective but not first line where adenotonsillectomy is an option, because of compliance problems. Adherence in children is around 50%, though respiratory therapist support improves compliance.

Indications: children who do not respond adequately to surgery, or in whom surgery is contraindicated — which can include patient and family preference, for example religious belief against surgery or blood transfusion. Often used in children with neuromuscular or craniofacial disorders, or residual OSA after surgery.

Requires objective assessment of adherence. Attempt to improve adherence by addressing adverse effects and using behaviour modification techniques; if the child remains non-compliant, use an alternative treatment.

CPAP pressure changes over time in children because of growth and development, so periodic reassessment is required. Polysomnography is used for CPAP titration.

Medication

Intranasal corticosteroid. Indicated only for children with mild OSA (AHI <5/h), and not for moderate or severe disease. Effects: small reduction in AHI; improvement in symptoms of adenoidal hypertrophy including snoring and observed apnoea; demonstrated shrinkage of adenoidal tissue. Follow-up evaluation is needed as long-term effects are unknown.

Leukotriene receptor antagonists may help in mild cases or where allergic rhinitis contributes to airway inflam‐ mation.

Orthodontic treatment

InterventionDetail and evidence
Rapid maxillary expansion (RME)Indication: children with maxillary constriction and dental malocclusion. Mechanism: increases the transverse diameter of the hard palate by reopening the mid-palatal suture, using a fixed appliance with an expansion screw anchored on selected teeth; after 3–4 months of expansion, normally mineralised structure is rebuilt. Reported effect: significant improvement in OSA signs, symptoms and polysomnographic parameters, with a reported mean reduction of about 4.8 events/h (−70%)
Miniscrew-assisted rapid palatal expansion (MARPE)Together with RME, has been shown to increase nasal cavity volume, improve nasal airflow and reduce upper airway resistance — effects that can contribute to alleviating mild-to- moderate SDB symptoms such as snoring and obstructive events, by promoting healthy airway development
Mandibular advancementFor class II malocclusion correction; together with maxillary expansion, one of the two ortho-dontic treatments in children that may change airway dimension. In children and adolescents with posterior crossbite or maxillary constriction and retrognathia, maxillary expansion and mandibular advancement produce upper airway dimensional change and AHI decrease
RoleOrthodontics may be used where adenotonsillectomy is not suitable, or in combination with surgery for better outcomes, particularly in patients with craniofacial abnormality or residual OSA. Long-term follow-up and comprehensive assessment including sleep studies are import-ant
EXAM TRAP — THE HONESTEVIDENCEONEX PA NSION

Functional responses to orthopaedic change are highly variable. Expansion treatment does not always help: some patients do not improve and some may get worse after RME. In studies of bi-maxillary expansion (maxilla by RME, mandible by dental expansion), around one third of patients became objectively worse after expansion. There is also natural regression of symptoms in some children, which inflates uncontrolled before-and-after results. Expansion may therefore be considered only for selected patients, and — crucially — any intervention undertaken with the purpose of improving OSA needs the OSA to have been diagnosed by a physician. Presenting expansion as a reliable cure for paediatric OSA is the single easiest way to fail this section.

Positional therapy and other measures

AHI is increased in the supine position, and lowest when children sleep prone— but position is difficult to main‐ tain overnight in a child.

Weight management for obese children.

Watchful waiting with close monitoring may be appropriate for mild snoring or mild OSA.

Myofunctional therapy as an adjunct.

Differences from adult SDB

Clinical key point

Paediatric scoring and management depend on age, clinical context and local sleep-laboratory methods. Fixed age transitions and adult severity bands should not be applied uncritically.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 341 WORDS
ChildrenAdults

LEARNING OUTCOME §8.9

Underlying syndromes and conditions

Clinical key point

High-risk groups, including Down syndrome, craniofacial syndromes, obesity and neuromuscular disease, warrant lower threshold for specialist assessment because symptoms may not reflect severity.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 460 WORDS

Medical conditions in children

Obesity, asthma, allergic rhinitis, gastro-oesophageal reflux disease and neuromuscular disorders all contribute to airway obstruction, inflammation or decreased airway muscle tone, increasing the risk and severity of SDB. Children with neuromuscular diseases such as Duchenne muscular dystrophy and myasthenia gravis have weakened respiratory muscles, leading to hypoventilation and greater susceptibility to SDB.

Genetic syndromes

Children with Down syndrome, Prader–Willi syndrome, Crouzon syndrome and cleft lip and palate often have craniofacial abnormalities — midfacial hypoplasia, macroglossia, retrognathia — that narrow the airway and predispose to OSA. In Prader–Willi syndrome, hypersomnolence and varying degrees of airway obstruction contribute to SDB.

HIGH-YIELD — DOWNSYNDROME

OSA is highly prevalent. Prevalence by AHI threshold: >1 in 69%; >1.5 in 76%; >2 in 75%;>5 (moderate–severe) in 50%; >10 in 34%. Compare 1–5% in the general paediatric population.

Predisposing factors: macroglossia, midface hypoplasia, overweight and poor muscle tone.

Moderate-to-severe OSA is more prevalent at younger ages.

All children with Down syndrome should be referred to a paediatric sleep laboratory for polysomnography by age 4(AAPD recommendation).

Clinical symptoms are unreliable: only a weak correlation exists between parental perception of sleep problems and polysomnographic results, and 53.8% of children with OSA had no symptoms. This is the single strongest argument for objective screening in a syndromic population.

In children, SDB with underlying conditions can lead to daytime behavioural problems, cognitive impairment, cardiovascular strain, growth delay and reduced quality of life. Early diagnosis and management are crucial, especially because some signs of neuromuscular disease may present initially as sleep-disordered breathing. Paediatricians and specialists must monitor at-risk children closely for snoring, mouth breathing, nocturnal gasping and behavioural problems.

Referral and diagnostic pathways

Clinical key point

Paediatric referral involves the child’s physician/sleep team and often ENT, orthodontic and other specialists. If maxillary constriction coexists, expansion may be an adjunct in a selected child but its effect on OSA is uncertain and needs objective follow-up.

Diagnosis of paediatric SDB by a physician

Clinical evaluation: detailed history focusing on habitual snoring, observed apnoeas, laboured or paradoxical breathing during sleep, daytime sleepiness, behavioural problems and learning difficulties. Physical examination attends to upper airway anatomy including tonsil and adenoid size, craniofacial features, and signs of obesity or comorbidity. Validated questionnaires aid screening but are not definitive.

Polysomnography is the diagnostic gold standard— overnight, attended, in-laboratory. It records EEG, EOG, EMG, ECG, respiratory airflow through oronasal sensors, respiratory effort via chest and abdominal belts, oxygen saturation, body position and (in children) end-tidal CO 2, allowing correlation of respiratory events with sleep

stages and arousals. An obstructive AHI of≥1 event per hour is abnormal in children.

Indications for PSG: clinical symptoms of SDB, especially before adenotonsillectomy in children under 2 years, in obesity, in craniofacial or neuromuscular disorders, in Down syndrome, or where symptoms persist after surgery. Also for CPAP titration and evaluation of complex cases.

Other tools: respiratory polygraphy records cardiorespiratory parameters but lacks sleep staging and is less valid‐ ated in children; nocturnal oximetry is a simple screening tool with good predictive value in high-risk children; home sleep apnoea testing is still emerging in paediatrics and is not widely recommended as a standalone diagnostic tool; daytime nap studies are less accurate; radiological imaging and biomarkers show promise but require further research.

EXAM TRAP — CBCTFORAI RWAY A N A LY SISINCHILDREN

The reliability and validity of CBCT airway analysis are both questionable. Reliability: the oropharynx is clearly imaged, but the most frequent sites of collapse are the nasopharynx and hypopharynx — and the adenoid site in children — so reliability is poor for the region that matters. Validity: upper airway dynamic function is very complex, and a healthy subject with a narrow airway can overcome anatomical risk by actively dilating the airway during inspiration. Cautions: poor inter-examiner reliability; normative values with large standard deviations; and, most fundamentally, airways are not static. Do not offer CBCT airway analysis as a diagnostic tool for paediatric OSA.

SELF-TEST — TOPIC 8

1. A 5-year-old snores nightly, is hyperactive at school and wets the bed. What is your assessment and plan?

This is a classic paediatric SDB presentation — behavioural rather than sleepy, with secondary enuresis in a previously dry child. I would take a medical, dental and family history covering allergies, asthma, recurrent ear and throat infections, prematurity, syndromic conditions and family history of sleep apnoea; record height, weight and neck size and plot growth, looking for failure to thrive; administer the Paediatric Sleep Questionnaire (positive if >0.33, that is more than 8 of 22 items) and consider the FAIREST-6; and perform an oral and craniofacial examination assessing tonsil size by the Brodsky scale, modified Mallampati, palatal vault and arch width, jaw size and position, tongue posture and tie, and breathing habits. I would then refer to a paediatric sleep physician or ENT specialist for comprehensive evaluation and polysomnography, noting that an obstructive AHI ≥1/h is abnormal in a child and that adenotonsillectomy is first-line if adenotonsillar hypertrophy is confirmed. I would not perform CBCT airway analysis, and I would not commence orthodontic expansion for the purpose of treating OSA before a physician diagnosis.

2. Give the paediatric AHI thresholds and explain why they differ from adult values.

Normal <1, mild 1 to <5, moderate 5 to <10, severe ≥10 — approximately one third of the adult values of 5, 15 and 30. An obstructive AHI of ≥1 event per hour is abnormal in a child. The lower thresholds reflect that children normally have very stable breathing during sleep, so even a small number of obstructive events represents pathology, and that the consequences — on growth, neurocognitive development, behaviour and academic performance — occur at much lower event rates. The practical trap is that a child with an AHI of 6 has moderate disease, not mild. Twelve years is the cut-off above which adult criteria are generally applied.

3. What is first-line treatment for paediatric OSA, and what are the risk factors for failure?

Adenotonsillectomy — the most effective surgical therapy, improving polysomnographic parameters, behaviour and quality of life. However, post-operative persistence of OSA is common, and adenotonsillectomy alone may be insufficient because it does not address oropharyngeal obstruction secondary to tonsillar hyperplasia, especially in children with Down syndrome, craniofacial anomalies or neuromuscular disease. Risk factors for failure: obesity; high pre-operative AHI; age under 3 or 7 and over; nasal abnormality or craniofacial disproportion; family history of OSA; and asthma. Post-operative polysomnography is suggested for these high-risk children even without persistent snoring. Consider overnight admission where the SpO 2 nadir is below 80%, the AHI is 24/h or more, or peak PCO 2 is 60 mmHg or more.

4. A parent asks whether palatal expansion will cure their child's sleep apnoea. How do you answer?

Honestly and with the evidence. Rapid maxillary expansion increases the transverse diameter of the hard palate by reopening the mid-palatal suture, and both RME and MARPE increase nasal cavity volume, improve nasal airflow and reduce upper airway resistance; reported mean AHI reductions of around 4.8 events per hour (about 70%) have been published in children with maxillary constriction. But functional responses to orthopaedic change are highly variable: expansion does not always help, some children do not improve and some get worse, and in bi-maxillary expansion studies about one third of patients became objectively worse. There is also natural regression of symptoms in some children, which flatters uncontrolled results. Expansion should therefore be considered only for selected patients, as an adjunct or where adenotonsillectomy is unsuitable, and any intervention intended to improve OSA requires the diagnosis to have been made by a physician, with long-term follow-up including sleep studies.

5. Distinguish the Mallampati and Brodsky classifications.

The modified Mallampati classification grades oral airway patency in relation to tongue position: class I, soft palate, uvula, fauces and pillars fully visible; II, soft palate and most of the uvula; III, soft palate and base of uvula only; IV, hard palate only. It is performed with the patient upright, mouth wide open and tongue maximally protruded without phonation. The Brodsky scale grades tonsil size as a percentage of the oropharyngeal width between the anterior pillars: grade 0, tonsils within the pillars; 1, <25%; 2, 25–50%; 3, 50–75%; 4, >75% or meeting in the midline. Assess by depressing the tongue and asking the patient to say "ah". Obstruction of more than 50% warrants further evaluation — but tonsil size does not correlate with OSA severity, so the grading identifies a risk factor, not a severity measure.

6. Why should a child with Down syndrome be referred for polysomnography even if the parents report no sleep problems?

Because clinical symptoms are unreliable in this population. OSA prevalence in Down syndrome is very high — approximately 50% at an AHI above 5, and 69% at an AHI above 1, compared with 1–5% in the general paediatric population — driven by macroglossia, midface hypoplasia, overweight and poor muscle tone, with moderate-to-severe disease more prevalent at younger ages. Only a weak correlation exists between parental perception of sleep problems and polysomnograph‐ ic findings, and 53.8% of affected children had no symptoms. Guidance therefore recommends that all children with Down syndrome be referred to a paediatric sleep laboratory for polysomnography by age 4, regardless of reported symptoms.

7. How do children with SDB differ from adults in presentation?

Children present with behaviour rather than sleepiness: hyperactivity, aggression, impulsivity, irritability, inattention and poor academic performance — a picture that closely mimics ADHD — together with restless sleep, unusual sleeping positions such as sitting up or hyperextending the neck, secondary bed-wetting, mouth breathing, morning headache and either excessive or poor weight gain. Adults present with loud chronic snoring, witnessed apnoeas, morning headache and dry mouth, daytime drowsiness, reduced libido and impaired memory. Causes also differ — adenotonsillar hypertrophy in children versus obesity, age and male sex in adults — as do thresholds, first-line treatment (adenotonsillectomy versus CPAP or an oral appliance), and prognosis: early treatment often resolves the condition entirely in children, whereas adult OSA is a chronic condition requiring lifelong management.
