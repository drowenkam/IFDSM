LEARNING OUTCOME §10.1

Valid informed consent

Clinical key point

Valid informed consent is a two-way process covering diagnosis, uncertainty, expected benefits, likely limitations, alternative treatment (including no therapy) and material harms. Document the patient’s questions and choices.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 704 WORDS
DEFINITION

Simple consent entails that a patient — or surrogate — with decision-making capacity freely authorises a treatment plan aimed at a mutually acknowledged treatment goal. The authorisation is " informed" when the clinician discloses, and the patient understands, the diagnosis, the relevant options for treatment including no treatment, and the respective risks and benefits of each.

Figure 10.1 — The three overlapping purposes of informed consent. Legal— protection from assault, and preventing unwanted pro‐ cedures. Ethical— protecting autonomous decision-making, and supporting patient-defined goals. Administrative compliance— docu‐ menting that the parties were involved in the informed-consent process, and providing efficient safeguards to ensure nominal fulfilment of ethical and legal requirements.

Why it is examinable: this figure distinguishes consent as a process from consent as a form. The administrative circle — mere nominal fulfilment — is the one that a signed form alone satisfies. Examiners want candidates who understand that all three purposes must be met.

Figure 10.2 — Suggestions for optimising clinical informed consent. Develop a practice of involving patients in decisions that is sensitive to patients' preferences for information and their decision-making styles, consistently applied to all patients, designed to substantively address not only the risks of care but also the expected benefits, relevant alternatives and what to anticipate before and after the procedure, and designed to ensure the decision-making capacity of the patient or surrogate, a voluntary choice free of undue influence, and comprehension — for example by asking patients to repeat what they heard. Explicitly establish the goals of care and prioritise them in the context of the patient's other life goals: commonly understood goals may require little clarification, while more explicit discussion is needed as decisions become more complex. Recognise that the informed-consent process serves more than legal purposes — legal (to protect patient rights), ethical (to support autonomous self-determination and decision-making), administrative compliance (to promote efficiency in health care) and interpersonal (to build the trust necessary to proceed with therapeutic intervention) — and allow the process sufficient flexibility to fulfil its varied purposes. Document the process thoroughly, using an electronic medical record wherever possible, and recognise that this may require more than one approach depending on local legal, ethical and compliance standards: procedure-specific consent forms, patient education materials (written and electronic), narrative notes describing the informed-consent process and the goals of care, and decision aids for particularly complex decisions.

Why it is examinable: the four purposes — legal, ethical, administrative, interpersonal— and the three tests of valid consent: capacity, voluntariness and comprehension. Note the practical technique of asking patients to repeat back what they heard; naming it demonstrates that you understand comprehension must be verified, not assumed.

What must be disclosed in dental sleep medicine

The nature of the sleep-related breathing disorder and its consequences if untreated.

The purpose of the proposed treatment and the alternatives— PAP therapy, positional therapy, weight loss, surgery, and no treatment.

The potential risks and consequences of appliance therapy, and the expected longevity of the appliance.

Possible dental side effects, before starting therapy— at minimum the chance of reduction in overjet and over‐ bite leading to a suboptimal occlusion, with fewer occlusal contact points, possible unilateral or bilateral crossbite in the (pre)molar region, and altered aesthetics.

The requirement for long-term follow-up.

HIGH-YIELD — THE PROCEDURALREQUIREMENTS

Explain the proposed treatment, with long-term follow-up, and obtain the patient's consent preferably in writing, be‐ fore fitting the appliance.

The consent form should clearly indicate the potential and probable side effects of using the appliance and its longev‐ ity.

Give the patient an opportunity to ask questions about the risks of treatment, and educate them about the risks of no treatment.

The patient signs in the presence of the qualified dentist or another team member; the dentist then countersigns and dates the document, which is kept as part of the record of care.

Give the patient a copy.

Document the process thoroughly using the electronic record, procedure-specific consent forms, patient education materials and narrative notes describing the process and the goals of care.

Signed, informed consent is mandatory— and, in the context of predictable occlusal change, it is your principal pro‐ tection.

Short/long-term side effects

Clinical key point

Dry mouth, salivation changes, dental tenderness and transient muscular symptoms may arise early. Long-term overjet, overbite and occlusal changes are sufficiently relevant to discuss before treatment rather than only after they occur.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 1,145 WORDS
THE O N E - SENTENCESUMMA RY

Most side effects of appliance treatment are mild and transient and disappear during the first few months of treatment — but in approximately 7.5% of patients they lead to termination of therapy, most often because of TMJ pain(it is less com‐ mon to give up because of occlusal change). Side effects contribute to about 20–30% of patients discontinuing therapy over‐ all.

Occlusal change — the quantitative detail

THE NUMBERS ONBITECHANGE

Direction of force: appliances produce distally directed forces on the upper dentition and forwardly directed forces on the lower teeth. Over time there is a dose-dependent shift in occlusion, with lower teeth moving anteriorly, upper teeth moving distally, and a loss of chewing contact area posteriorly.

Magnitude: reduced overjet ~1.9 mm and overbite ~2.3 mm; decreased overjet and overbite during the first 5 years; around one third of patients experience more than 1 mm of change. At 17 years, median overjet change −1.1 mm and median overbite change −1.6 mm. Average decrease of ~2.5 mm in overjet and overbite is described, which may be favourable or unfavourable.

Frequency: approximately 80% of patients who use an appliance every night over 7 years will experience occlusal change. 85% had bite change detectable on study models and cephalograms — though some bites do not change. The proportion developing occlusal change increased over the first 2 years to 13.3% of cases and remained relatively constant thereafter.

Time course: overbite decreases less with time; overjet decreases continuously at a constant rate. Initial overjet has no effect on the pattern, but initial overbite does: with a smaller initial overbite the rate of change first decreases then increases after ~9 years, whereas a deep bite decreases gradually with time. A larger initial overjet is associated with a greater total reduction in both overjet and overbite. Bite changes increase progressively as long as treatment continues.

Other occlusal changes: lower arch expansion, with increased lower intercanine and intermolar distance and decreased lower crowding — intercanine width increases continuously while intermolar width increases rapidly then tapers off; opening of interdental spaces; 51% posterior open bite(loss of occlusal contact on at least two posterior teeth); 62% anterior crossbite of at least one tooth; increased maximal opening.

Anteroposterior relationship: a tendency toward mesio-occlusion compared with CPAP — that is, a change from class I to class III, or class II to class I or III. Also a tendency to unilateral or bilateral crossbite in the (pre)molar region, because with a mesial shift the broader part of the lower arch occludes with the narrower part of the upper arch. There is a significant decrease in the number of occlusal contacts in the (pre)molar region with both appliances and CPAP, attrib‐ uted to the incisal guidance phenomenon — but no tendency to anterior open bite.

Predictors: a significant association exists between change in overbite and the amount of mandibular protrusion, and initial AHI is positively related to the magnitude of overjet reduction— because more severe OSA requires more advancement for symptom relief. But the degree of advancement is not related to the magnitude of occlusal change in some analyses (any degree of advancement can produce change in predisposed people), change cannot be predicted from the pre-treatment occlusal classification, and patient age, sex and pre-treatment BMI are not related to occlusal change. The greatest vulnerability is within the first 2 years, after which change may partly reverse as a new occlusal equilibrium develops.

Mechanism: occlusal changes occur because of a failure to reposition the mandible during the day following use of the device, rather than as a result of immutable changes caused by the device overnight. Side effects are a product of protruding the mandible to achieve a therapeutic effect, rather than of specific appliance design.

CPAP is not innocent: CPAP also causes retroclination of the upper incisors, from posteriorly directed forces on the anterior maxilla by a tightly fitted nasal mask, and long-term use of both CPAP and appliances produces significant dental change.

What patients actually experience

In most cases occlusal changes are minor and not noticed by the patient; only a minority notice bite change. Most patients are unaware, are not caused problems, and do not want to stop using the appliance.

Where noticed, symptoms of bite change are: gaps and food impaction; occasional lisping when talking; and — rarely — difficulty chewing on the back teeth or tearing food with the front teeth.

Most patients do not lose posterior occlusion.

Position of professional bodies: for most people the change in bite is not annoying, is not a risk to oral health, and does not require further dental treatment.

Most importantly— and this is the clinically consequential point — these bite changes influence the mechanism of the device, since a forward shift of the lower teeth relative to the upper results in a progressively reduced degree of effective mandibular advancement, putting the efficacy of treatment at risk.

Craniofacial and TMJ effects

Clinical key point

Mandibular advancement can affect teeth, periodontal loading, jaw muscles and TMJ function. Record baseline occlusion, joint symptoms and protrusive range to distinguish treatment-related change from pre-existing findings.

That sentence is worth being able to deploy — but only after you have documented that the patient understood and accepted the trade-off.

Morning guide and exercises

Clinical key point

Morning repositioning devices and exercises may help re-establish habitual bite contact after nightly wear, but do not guarantee prevention or complete reversal of long-term tooth movement.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 495 WORDS
W H AT AMORNINGOCCLUSALGUIDEISANDDOES

A morning occlusal guide(morning repositioning device, or "AM aligner") is a device used after waking to help realign the jaw and teeth after wearing a mandibular advancement device overnight. The patient bites into the guide for as long as it takes for the teeth to re-establish habitual occlusion, which can reverse tooth movement and reposition the condyles. It is used to reposition the mandible into its habitual pre-treatment position — and, just as importantly, it lets the patient know when occlusal change is occurring: if they cannot fit into the aligner with the back teeth in occlusion, bite change has oc‐ curred.

How it works

Realigns the bite— custom-made to fit the patient's upper and lower teeth, guiding them back to their original maximum intercuspation.

Exercises the jaw muscles— applying pressure exercises and stretches the muscles of mastication, which are tight after a night of wear.

Positions the condyles— assists in repositioning the condyles to their pre-treatment alignment, reducing strain and discomfort.

Monitors progress— the patient uses it each morning to check jaw alignment; if it does not fit, they should contact the practice.

Practical points

Suggest it for every patient in whom appliance therapy is commenced.

Two fabrication types: those using thermoacrylic beads, and those using a prefabricated sheet(AM aligner) placed in hot water, moulded around the teeth, with the patient asked to bite in centric occlusion.

Should be custom made and recorded in centric occlusion.

Check the occlusion before and after, with and without the morning aligner in place, to make sure the back teeth are in occlusion even with the aligner seated.

Wear for 15–20 minutes in the morning after waking— some protocols specify around 10 minutes.

Consider making one at the same appointment as the appliance impression by also taking a centric occlusion record (Topic 5.6).

Morning exercises

Certain jaw exercises help relieve stiffness of the masticatory muscles and accelerate repositioning of the mandible to its normal position after a night of appliance use. They may also inhibit or minimise occlusal functional changes in predisposed patients.

Perform slowly and gently: opening the mouth wide, moving the jaw left and right, resting the chin on a fist to let the jaw settle, and curling the tongue back to find the retruded position. These stretch the muscles of mastication and reduce discomfort.

Exercises increase contact between the teeth — good in the short term, with limited long-term evidence.

Both the guide and the exercises are used in the morning immediately after removing the appliance, with duration and frequency determined by the treating dentist.

Chewing gum also helps, mostly with acute changes in the morning.

These measures are recommended by the AADSM to prevent and manage common side effects such as changes in tooth position and jaw muscle pain — but they are not a cure-all: some tooth or root changes, though often clinic‐ ally insignificant, may still occur.

Managing side effects

Clinical key point

Manage mild symptoms with fit adjustment, slower titration, oral care and follow-up; investigate progressive joint pain, tooth mobility, tissue injury or significant occlusal change promptly. Tailor interventions to severity and patient preference.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 813 WORDS

Conservative management of occlusal change

Temporary cessation or reduction in use of the device, plus remedial exercises every morning following remov‐ al. With exercises, occlusal changes resolve within 2 weeks of treatment cessation in most cases.

Use of an occlusal guide.

Following satisfactory resolution, nightly treatment can resume with an ongoing exercise regimen as a prophy‐ lactic measure and careful monitoring.

It is reasonable to continue treatment in the presence of occlusal change, provided that it is regularly monitored (at least 6-monthly clinical reviews), is not associated with unacceptable symptoms, is not progressive, and there is adequate posterior occlusal support.

If conservative management fails — especially if there is loss of posterior occlusion— permanent treatment ces‐ sation may be necessary.

Correction of the occlusion with restorations, crowns and/or orthodontic treatment should be considered only if the patient has discontinued treatment and is able to use another OSA treatment, because the changes will contin‐ ue — albeit at a slower pace — as long as the patient wears the device.

Minimising occlusal change

Minimise titration— use the minimum advancement that relieves symptoms. Allow a temporary increase during a cold when snoring worsens, but remind the patient to titrate back

Cover all teeth— do not leave molars uncovered; use a hard rather than flexible material for better protection; include incisor coverage

For a patient with previous orthodontic treatment, consider alternating regimens: 4 nights with the appliance and 3 nights with the morning aligner, or an appliance at night and a retainer during the day, to reduce tooth movement

Reduce wearing time where appropriate; ask the patient to remove the appliance on waking; consider 1–2 nights off per week in vulnerable patients

Chewing gum in the morning

Review vulnerable patients early and keep a list of them

Morning aligner for every patient, custom-made in centric occlusion, worn 15–20 minutes

The dentist's role and follow-up

Monitoring and adjustment— a qualified dentist is essential for monitoring side effects and making necessary adjustments to the appliance or plan.

Palliative care— supportive care to manage discomfort, especially during the initial adaptation period.

Patient education— reassure that symptoms are likely temporary and manageable.

Regular follow-up— every 6 months to a year, to evaluate for and manage side effects appropriately, with documentation of presence, management and resolution, and communication to the general dentist and medical pro‐ vider.

Resolve issues and side effects early and promptly— this is the single strongest determinant of whether the patient stays in treatment.

Suspending or abandoning treatment

Clinical key point

Pause or stop OAT for serious harm, unsuitable dental support or unacceptable persistent symptoms; then coordinate alternative OSA treatment and verification to avoid leaving significant disease untreated.

Factors in the decision, and what comes next

Patient-specific factors— overall health, tolerance for side effects and commitment to therapy.

Regular follow-up— monitoring by qualified dentists is necessary to survey for dental side effects and occlusal change, and is essential for maximising adherence and effectiveness.

Device and settings— efficacy and side effects depend on appliance type, degree of protrusion and other design settings, so modification may be preferable to abandonment.

Alternatives: CPAP is the primary alternative, particularly for severe OSA, and can be considered when appliance therapy fails or is refused; combination therapy (appliance plus CPAP at lower pressure, or appliance plus positional therapy or EPAP); surgical intervention where other conservative treatments are unsuccessful or not tolerated; in a younger patient, orthognathic surgery may be considered; and for those who cannot accept ongoing occlusal change, a half-and-half regimen alternating CPAP and appliance on different nights.

Where treatment is stopped: notify the medical provider, and refer non-compliant patients back to the medical provider to consider alternative therapies, since untreated or subtherapeutic OSA carries long-term health risk.

Treatment of established occlusal change

Only a small group of patients need any treatment at all.

Leave it alone within the first 12–18 months— most will self-correct. This is why it is important to review vulnerable patients early, so that this option remains available.

If treatment is required: orthodontics; orthognathic surgery (very rare); optimising the occlusion by occlusal equilibration and restoration — refer to a prosthodontist if the patient simply wants to chew well; prosthodontic overlays.

Remember the rule from 10.5: definitive correction should be undertaken only once appliance therapy has been discontinued and the patient is on another OSA treatment, because change continues while the appliance is worn.

SELF-TEST — TOPIC 10

1. What must you disclose before fitting an oral appliance?

The nature of the sleep-related breathing disorder and the consequences of leaving it untreated; the purpose of the proposed treatment; all relevant alternatives including PAP therapy, positional therapy, weight loss, surgery and no treatment; the po‐ tential and probable risks; the expected longevity of the appliance; and the requirement for long-term follow-up. Specifically on dental side effects, at minimum the chance of reduction in overjet and overbite leading to a suboptimal occlusion with fewer occlusal contact points, possible unilateral or bilateral crossbite in the (pre)molar region, and altered aesthetics. Consent should be obtained in writing before fitting, the form should state the potential and probable side effects and the appliance's longevity, the patient should have the opportunity to ask questions, they sign in the presence of the dentist or a team member, the dentist countersigns and dates, the patient is given a copy, and the process is documented in narrative notes alongside the form.

2. A patient asks whether the appliance will change their bite. Answer them honestly, with figures.

Yes, most likely — this is an expected consequence rather than a rare complication. Approximately 80% of patients using an appliance every night over seven years will experience occlusal change, and 85% show change detectable on study models and cephalograms. Typically overjet reduces by about 1.9 mm and overbite by about 2.3 mm, with around one third of patients exceeding 1 mm of change; at 17 years the median changes are −1.1 mm overjet and −1.6 mm overbite. However, in most cases the change is minor and not noticed: only a minority perceive it, most patients report no problems and do not wish to stop, and professional bodies consider that for most people the change is not annoying, not a risk to oral health, and does not require further dental treatment. Where noticed, symptoms are gaps and food impaction, occasional lisping, and rarely difficulty chewing on the back teeth. I would then explain the mitigation — a morning aligner, minimal titration, jaw exercises, possible nights off — and the monitoring schedule, and document that we had this conversation.

3. Which patients are most vulnerable to occlusal change?

Patients with an edge-to-edge bite and minimal overjet and overbite, who can easily become class III; younger patients, with more flexible bone and a longer expected duration of wear; those with a history of orthodontic treatment; those wearing the appliance more than eight hours or seven nights a week; those advanced beyond about 80% of maximum protrusion; those with a suboptimal therapeutic result, because they keep advancing the mandible chasing residual symptoms; and patients with severe OSA and obesity. Additionally, patients with high aesthetic requirements, narrow acceptance of occlusal change, or body dysmorphic features need particularly careful consent, because for them a clinically trivial change may be subjectively intolerable. I would keep a list of vulnerable patients and review them early — within the first 12–18 months, while spontaneous correction remains possible.

4. Describe the mechanism of occlusal change, and what it is not.

The appliance produces distally directed forces on the upper dentition and forwardly directed forces on the lower teeth. The main cause of the resulting change is tilting of teeth— retroclination of the upper incisors, proclination of the lower incisors, a mesial tendency in the molar relationship and an overall reduction in overjet. It is not due to a change in mandibular length, a change in skeletal pattern, or functional repositioning and neuromuscular adaptation — appliances cause tooth movement, not craniofacial skeletal change. A further important point is that the change reflects a failure to reposition the mandible during the day after use, rather than immutable overnight change, which is the rationale for morning aligners and exercises. Finally, side effects are a product of protruding the mandible to achieve a therapeutic effect rather than of any specific appliance design, and CPAP is not exempt: a tightly fitted nasal mask also produces retroclination of the upper incisors.

5. A patient on therapy for three years has developed a posterior open bite on two teeth. How do you manage this?

First establish the facts: compare against baseline records, models and photographs, quantify the change and determine whether it is progressive, whether it is symptomatic, and — critically — whether adequate posterior occlusal support re‐ mains. Then conservative management: temporary cessation or reduced use of the device together with remedial exercises every morning after removal, since occlusal changes resolve within about two weeks of cessation in most cases with exercises; and use of a morning occlusal guide. Following satisfactory resolution, nightly treatment can resume with an ongoing prophylactic exercise regimen and careful monitoring, minimising the titration to the least effective advancement. It is reasonable to continue treatment in the presence of occlusal change provided it is monitored at least 6-monthly, is not associated with unacceptable symptoms, is not progressive, and posterior support is adequate. If conservative management fails — particularly where posterior occlusion is being lost — permanent cessation may be necessary, but only with the patient accepting an alternative therapy from their physician or understanding and accepting the risks of stopping, with the physician informed. Definitive occlusal correction with restorations, crowns or orthodontics should be undertaken only after appliance therapy has been discontinued and the patient is on another OSA treatment, because the change continues while the appliance is worn.

6. A patient reports unilateral jaw pain three weeks after starting therapy. Work through the causes and manage‐ ment.

Unilateral pain points to asymmetry rather than to excessive advancement. Causes: prone (stomach) sleeping with pressure on one side of the jaw; the appliance not seating properly; imbalance within the appliance or a midline discrepancy; uneven occlusal contact; or asymmetrical mandibular protrusion. Management: institute a self-care programme; confirm the appliance is seating properly; retrude the screw mechanism on the affected side by 0.5–1 mm; adjust the appliance occlusion to gain bilateral contact; and if these fail, reset to a new bite registration. Contrast with bilateral pain, which suggests advancement beyond TMJ tolerance, a vertical dimension outside the jaw's tolerance, or an appliance that is too tight or too loose — managed by retruding the mandible for a few weeks then advancing gradually, modifying the vertical dimension, adjusting retention, reducing the titration rate, and supportive measures of rest, ice, soft diet and anti-inflammatories.

7. Describe the morning occlusal guide: purpose, fabrication and use.

Its purpose is to reposition the mandible into its habitual pre-treatment position after a night of advancement — realigning the bite toward original maximum intercuspation, exercising and stretching the tight muscles of mastication, and assisting repositioning of the condyles to their pre-treatment alignment. It also serves as a self-monitoring device: if the patient cannot fit into the aligner with the back teeth in occlusion, bite change has occurred and they should contact the practice. Fabrication is either from thermoacrylic beads or from a prefabricated sheet placed in hot water and moulded around the teeth with the patient biting in centric occlusion; it should be custom made and recorded in centric occlusion, and it is convenient to take the centric record at the same appointment as the appliance impression. Check the occlusion with and without the aligner to confirm the back teeth occlude with it seated. It is worn for 15–20 minutes on waking, immediately after removing the appliance, and should be suggested for every patient. Alongside it, gentle jaw exercises — wide opening, lateral movements, resting the chin on a fist, curling the tongue back to find the retruded position — and chewing gum help with acute morning change. These measures are recommended by the AADSM but are not a cure-all; some tooth and root change, though usually clinically insignificant, may still occur.
