Clinical guideline and diagnosis

Clinical key point

Before providing OAT, confirm the medical diagnosis and the agreed treatment pathway. The 2025 AADSM standards emphasise qualified dental assessment and in-person comprehensive examination, impressions and construction-bite records.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 1,338 WORDS

The governing document remains the joint AASM and AADSM Clinical Practice Guideline (Ramar et al., "an up‐ date for 2015"), supplemented by the AADSM Dental Sleep Medicine Standards, most recently updated in 2025. The 2015 document was the first official joint guideline of the two academies and is built around the collaborative physician–dentist model.

Diagnosis first

The presence or absence of OSA must be determined before initiating treatment with an appliance, for two reasons: to identify patients at risk from the complications of sleep apnoea, and to provide a baseline against which the effectiveness of subsequent treatment can be established.

Detailed diagnostic criteria for OSA are available and include clinical signs, symptoms and the findings identified by polysomnography.

The severity of sleep-related respiratory problems must be established in order to make an appropriate treatment decision — and severity is predictive of the response to an appliance.

Appliance fitting and who may do it

Oral appliances should be fitted by qualified dental personnel who are trained and experienced in the overall care of oral health, the temporomandibular joint, dental occlusion and associated oral structures.

Dental management of patients with appliances should be overseen by practitioners who have undertaken serious training in sleep medicine and/or sleep-related breathing disorders, with focused emphasis on the proper protocol for diagnosis, treatment and follow-up. The guideline states that the dentist should receive diplomate certification in dental sleep medicine or seek the designation of "qualified dentist".

Although cephalometric evaluation is not always required for a patient who will use an oral appliance, appropriately trained professionals should perform these examinations when they are deemed necessary.

Why it is examinable: nasolaryngoscopic assessment is how a candidate demonstrates that appliance selection is anatomically reasoned rather than empirical. Jaw thrust sometimes increases space at only one or two of the four levels — which is exactly the information you want before committing a patient to mandibular advancement. The Müller manoeuvre(the patient inhales while the clinician obstructs the nose, reducing intrapharyngeal pressure) demonstrates which levels collapse, and can be combined with jaw thrust. Note the caution: jaw thrust produces an oblique stretch on the pharyngeal constrictors, increasing wall tension and therefore compliance and collapsibility — so the manoeuvre is not a purely benign predictor.

Upper-airway mechanisms and predictors

Clinical key point

Airway response is variable: anatomy, compliance, mandibular range, vertical opening and comfort matter. A larger protrusion is not invariably better if it creates pain or prevents all-night use.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 755 WORDS

Mechanism

The primary mode of action is improvement in passive pharyngeal anatomy. Mandibular advancement reduces pharyngeal collapsibility in a dose-dependent manner without systematically changing genioglossus muscle function, in a predominantly obese, severe-OSA population.

The appliance advances the mandible and changes the volume of the upper airway. The airway enlarges — particularly in its lateral dimension at the velopharyngeal level— and the tongue is displaced anteriorly. There is an increase in the velopharyngeal, nasopharyngeal and anteroposterior dimensions of the upper airway.

Appliances improve pharyngeal collapsibility while loop gain, arousal threshold and dilator muscle activity are unchanged.

Dose dependence extends beyond anatomy: there is dose-dependent enlargement of the upper airway, augmentation of upper airway muscle activity and improvement in minimum SaO 2 with increasing mandibular protrusion, and the

respiratory and sleep variables also improve in a dose-dependent way.

Patients with mild OSA are thought to be better suited to the mechanism than those with more severe disease: they generally have less collapsible airways, and the pharynx increases more, along its whole length, than in patients with more severe disease.

The mechanism is less predictable than that of CPAP, even in the longer term. An appliance can be used alone or in combination with CPAP or positional therapy where it is not fully successful alone, and can reduce the side effects of CPAP in patients troubled by uncomfortably high pressures.

Efficacy and effect

Success rate up to ~80% in mild OSA; ~60% in more severe cases

related to insufficient apnoea control and poor adherence— a useful clinical marker

Long-term efficacy is good to about 10 years, but real-world long-term effectiveness probably declines because the natural history of the disease is deterioration in AHI

Effect on daytime sleepiness is unclear in snorers and mild-to-moderate OSA; in more severe OSA there is a reduction compared with placebo, but CPAP is more effective. The low-arousal-threshold subgroup may experience more subjective advantage from an appliance

Reduces AHI and improves nightly oxygenation

Increases the percentage of slow-wave sleep; increases REM sleep duration

Health effects: reduces blood pressure (similar effect to CPAP), improves the nightly dips in blood pressure, and improves endothelial and cardiac function. Improves mean sleep latency on the MSLT

Snoring is controlled more effectively by an appliance than by an intraoral placebo, though CPAP is more effective than an appliance. Persistent snoring during appliance treatment has been

Indications and contraindications

Clinical key point

Device designs and materials

Clinical key point

Choose an effective, appropriately regulated custom adjustable device, weighing bulk, durability, repairability, adjustability, material tolerance and lateral movement. Avoid design selection by marketing claims alone.

COMPLETE TEACHING LESSON · 1,494 WORDS
EXAM TRAP — THE BOIL - A N D - BITEDEVICE

Do not use off-the-shelf, boil-and-bite, non-individualised appliances. They sit in a fixed position and cannot be titrated reliably, fit poorly with inadequate retention, are poorly tolerated, and carry the potential for undesirable dental side effects. A boil-and-bite device also fails the AADSM definition of an effective appliance on at least three counts — reliable titration, verifiable setting and stable retention. Self-fitted appliances are, at most, temporary devices; they do not fit well and cannot be worn long term.

Why it is examinable: this is the same taxonomy expressed as attached versus unattached and traction versus compression. The two clinically important consequences are that traction devices impose lower stress than compression devices — which produce greater early masticatory myalgia and residual tongue pain — and that unattached (interlocking) designs permit the mandible to drop in the supine position, which may reduce efficacy.

Design principles that affect outcome

The most common and most effective devices are custom-made, titratable appliances that do not allow mouth opening. They comprise an upper and a lower part with an intermaxillary adjustment mechanism allowing the mandible to be advanced successively to find an optimal position. The two parts may be fixed to one another by, for example, a screw mechanism, or the mandible may be allowed to move laterally and/or vertically.

Appliances that allow mouth opening are less effective at reducing sleep apnoea; adding elastics to prevent opening halved apnoea frequency in the supine position. Elastics increase MAD success in positional OSA. A fixed device may therefore be better than a loose one, because the mandible drops supine.

Larger advancement gives larger efficacy; smaller vertical opening is preferred— increasing the vertical open‐ ing increases pharyngeal collapsibility and has been associated with reduced appliance efficacy.

Coverage matters for side effects. Fewer changes in overjet and overbite were seen with a soft elastomeric device covering parts of the alveolar processes in addition to all the teeth, compared with a hard acrylic appliance with full occlusal coverage fixed mainly to the teeth. However, hard materials provide better protection against tooth movement, and all teeth need to be covered — do not leave molars uncovered, or they will move or over-erupt. A flexible appliance without incisor coverage increased lower anterior irregularity compared with a rigid appliance with incisor coverage. A specific orthodontic appliance incorporating forces to counteract the posteriorly directed load on the upper front teeth had a positive effect on overjet change.

Between-device differences are clinically small. There are clinically insignificant differences between appliances in symptoms or AHI. The overall conclusion is that the influence of device design on efficacy and side effects is al‐ most unknown, and that side effects are a product of protruding the mandible to achieve a therapeutic effect rather than of specific appliance design.

Designs not recommended: those marketed on theories of airway and bone remodelling, promoted by manufactur‐ ers, with no peer-reviewed scientific publication, no demonstrated superiority over current appliances, and non- adherence to AADSM guidance — typically bulky and not titratable.

Fabrication techniques

Traditional: dental casts and bite registrations used to create custom appliances. Well established, allowing precise custom fitting to the patient's anatomy.

3D printing / additive manufacturing: digital models built layer by layer. High customisation, potentially reduced production time and cost, and in-house printing offering fast replacement. DLP and SLA offer advantages in accuracy, resolution and speed; the choice depends on the application and the desired workflow.

Digital scanning is more accurate than conventional impressions. Check the scan before and after for a correct midline.

Construction bite registration

Clinical key point

Take a reproducible construction-bite registration in the patient’s tolerable protrusive range and record the position. There is no universally best starting percentage; incremental adjustment should balance efficacy and comfort.

The rationale, and why not maximum protrusion

The acceptable range of mandibular advancement for an individual patient is not known. Maximum protrusion pro‐ duces the greatest airway enlargement, but this is not an adequate basis for setting the appliance because it causes TMJ and muscle discomfort.

Classical practice: occlusal registration at 60% of maximum mandibular protrusion with 2 mm of vertical sep‐ aration of the upper and lower incisors. Other conventions advance the mandible to about 75% of maximum protrusion, or take a wax interocclusal record with the mandible in the most protrusive position the patient can comfortably maintain. A practical rule is to start at 50% and do not go beyond 75%.

A previously proposed concept of a consistent between-patient "threshold" of 70% of maximal comfortable ad‐ vancement for optimal outcome has been challenged by the observation that airway collapsibility improves in non- responders during the initial range of advancement (0–50%), whereas in responders the majority of improvement in collapsibility occurs close to the maximum possible advancement. In other words, the dose–response curve differs between phenotypes.

Excessive protrusion does not guarantee improved efficacy, may affect compliance, and in some instances overpro‐ trusion may worsen the sleep-disordered breathing.

The degree of subsequent bite change is influenced by appliance design, the initial type of bite and the treatment duration — as well as by the amount of protrusion.

Using the George Gauge — a step-by-step method

Determine the protrusive range. Record the most retruded and the most protruded readings and add their absolute values. For example, −3 and +7 gives a range of 10 mm; −4 and +5 gives 9 mm.

Calculate the target. Take the bite at 50–70% of that range. For 60% of 10 mm = 6 mm: starting from −3, the bite is registered at +3.

Select the bite fork for vertical opening. The 2 mm grey fork is the usual choice. The 5 mm white fork is usually too much opening — it promotes mouth breathing and inhibits lip seal.

Seat and check. Ensure the blue incisal guide clamps the lower incisors firmly. Check the midline: it should be the same and reproducible at centric and at protrusion.

Register. Do not inject bite registration material into the area of the lower incisor clamp.

Check posterior vertical clearance, especially in a deep bite case. If clearance is insufficient — usually at the second molars — ask the laboratory not to cover the sevens.

Consider a centric occlusion record as well, for fabrication of a morning aligner (Topic 10).

Requirements for an accurate impression

Accuracy and detail reproduction— some materials reproduce detail as fine as 20–25 µm

Ease of handling and setting— easy to mix and manipulate, with a suitable working time but a short setting time to minimise discomfort

Dimensional stability— shape and size must remain consistent from removal until the cast is poured

Appropriate viscosity and thixotropy— held in the tray, yet fluid enough to adapt to tissues and capture detail

Elasticity and tear strength— must stretch and recover without tearing on removal, especially around undercuts and deep grooves

A rigid tray for support

Wettability— ability to spread over and contact moist oral tissues, crucial for detail capture

Moisture control— proper isolation of the working area, since moisture interferes with wetting and detail capture

Biocompatibility— non-toxic, non-irritant, safe intraorally

Clinical technique— the operator's knowledge and application of the chosen technique matter as much as the material

SELF-TEST — TOPIC 5

1. State the six recommendations of the 2015 AASM/AADSM oral appliance guideline.

(i) Sleep physicians should prescribe an oral appliance, rather than no treatment, for adults who request treatment for primary snoring without OSA. (ii) Where an adult with OSA is intolerant of CPAP or prefers alternative therapy, sleep physicians should prescribe an oral appliance rather than no treatment. (iii) Qualified dentists should use a custom, titratable appliance over non-custom oral devices. (iv) Sleep physicians should conduct follow-up sleep testing to improve or confirm treatment efficacy, rather than follow up without testing. (v) Sleep physicians and qualified dentists should instruct patients to return for periodic office visits with both. (vi) Qualified dentists should provide oversight of appliance therapy to survey for dental- related side effects and occlusal changes and reduce their incidence.

2. Walk me through taking a construction bite with a George Gauge.

First establish the protrusive range by recording the most retruded and most protruded readings and adding their absolute values — for example −3 and +7 gives 10 mm. Calculate the target as 50–70% of that range; 60% of 10 mm is 6 mm, so starting from −3 the bite is registered at +3. Select the vertical: the 2 mm grey fork is standard, since the 5 mm white fork usually gives too much opening and promotes mouth breathing while inhibiting lip seal. Check the blue incisal guide clamps the lower incisors firmly, and check the midline — it should be reproducible at both centric and protrusion, though not necessarily identical. Register the bite, avoiding injecting material into the lower incisor clamp area. Then check posterior vertical clearance, particularly in a deep bite, and if clearance is inadequate at the second molars ask the laboratory not to cover the sevens. Consider also taking a centric occlusion record for a morning aligner.

3. Why not simply set the appliance at maximum protrusion?

Maximum protrusion produces the greatest airway enlargement, but it is not an adequate basis for the setting because it causes TMJ and muscle discomfort, threatens compliance, and increases dental side effects. Excessive protrusion has not been shown to guarantee improved efficacy and in some instances overprotrusion may worsen the sleep-disordered breathing. The ac‐ ceptable range for an individual patient is not known, so we start at 50–70% of maximum (25–75% is within the comfort and therapeutic range) and titrate incrementally to the minimum effective position — leaving some leeway rather than going to 75% immediately. The dose–response relationship also differs between phenotypes: in responders most of the improvement in collapsibility occurs near maximal advancement, whereas in non-responders collapsibility improves early, over the initial 0– 50%.

4. Name and describe the six appliance design categories.

Lateral Push— bilateral metal Herbst rods pushing the mandibular overlay forward against the maxillary anchor, titrated by embedded displacement screws; robust with fine titration but metal may impinge on soft tissue. Lateral Pull— bilateral elast‐ omeric or nylon straps pulling the mandibular overlay forward, titrated by swapping strap lengths; small but the strap fractures and titration is gross. Interlocking Dorsal— angled dorsal posts from the mandibular overlay interlocking with maxillary blocks, titrated by screws in the blocks (10 turns = +1 mm); unattached, so the mandible can drop. Precision Post— dual per‐ pendicular interlocking posts on both overlays, titrated by swapping an overlay for one at a new setting. Mono/Twin Block— overlays fused (mono) or mated by interlocking blocks (twin), titrated by swapping overlays; restricts jaw movement and may cause TMJ discomfort. Anterior Pull— an anterior screw or strap repositioning the mandible against the opposing overlay; ideal for poor dexterity as adjustment is accessible at the front, with fine titration, but it concentrates force on the anterior teeth and increases vertical dimension. Expressed differently, the same taxonomy divides into attached versus unattached and traction versus compression, with traction devices imposing lower stress.

5. Compare nylon and acrylic appliances.

Nylon (polyamide, usually CAD-CAM) is lighter, smaller and more robust with less breakage, making it preferable for bruxers; it retains by flexion into undercuts, needs less posterior clearance (~3 mm), and has a visible titration scale making adjustment easier. Its drawbacks are that it stains readily so polishing is important, titration is gross at 0.5–1 mm, and it cannot be relined or repaired — a large new restoration means a remake. Acrylic retains by frictional resistance, can be relined and repaired, and permits finer screw adjustment bit by bit, but breaks more readily in bruxers and needs more clearance (~5 mm). Cobalt-chrome is extremely durable and good for bruxers but expensive to cast and limited to framework designs.

6. What do you check at the delivery appointment?

Six stages. Pre-delivery: verify the prescription against the plan, inspect for bubbles, rough edges, distortion and smooth screw operation, then disinfect and lubricate. Seating and retention: seat posteriorly first, ask the patient to swallow, say "pa–ta–ka" and open wide, then apply gentle bilateral traction — it should resist displacement without discomfort; adjust acrylic in stress- bearing areas only. Vertical dimension: measure with a Willis gauge or from a fixed landmark with and without the appliance, confirm the 2–4 mm interincisal increase is tolerated and that the relaxed jaw has no strain. Protrusion: verify against the millimetre scale, check even bilateral posterior contact with no slide back to retrusion, and observe swallowing and speaking. Comfort and soft tissue: check pressure points, gag triggers, mucosal blanching, ridge soreness, gingival clearance and premature contacts. Finally: adjust and polish, demonstrate insertion and removal over a soft surface, give written home care instructions and warranty signed by patient and team member, provide emergency contact details, and book review within 30 days.

7. Your patient has a deep bite with a marked curve of Spee. What are the implications?

A deep curve of Spee means there is insufficient vertical clearance for the appliance at the second molars. The options are to increase the vertical dimension — which is undesirable, since greater vertical opening increases pharyngeal collapsibility and reduces efficacy, and promotes mouth breathing — or, preferably, to instruct the laboratory not to cover the second molars. The trade-off is that uncovered molars may move or over-erupt, so this must be documented, explained to the patient and monitored at review. Material choice also helps: nylon requires only about 3 mm of clearance compared with about 5 mm for acrylic. Note separately that an anterior open bite precludes a mandibular advancement device altogether, because there is no posterior clearance at all.
